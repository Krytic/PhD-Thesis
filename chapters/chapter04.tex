\chapter{Detailed Models of Overcontact Binary Stars}\label{chap:close_contact}

\chapterEpigraph{The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars, but in ourselves.}{Julius Caesar, Act I, Scene II}

\lettrine[lines=3]{A}{s previously discussed, stars} undergoing mass transfer will generally experience \gls{rlof} during their lifetimes. In a binary where both stars are undergoing mass transfer, both stars may undergo \gls{rlof} simultaneously, resulting in a contact binary system in which both stars physically touch. Contact binaries are not uncommon -- they form one in every approximately 100 main-sequence stars \citep{Rucinski:1998} and 39 in every 100 binary systems \citep{Green:2023}.

The evolution is also complex. Sufficiently close binaries may overflow their $\mathcal{L}_2$ points, or create mergers on exceptionally short timescales -- typically $P_\text{orb}\lesssim\SI{2}{\day}$, but periods as low as $P_\text{orb}=\SI{85}{\minute}$ \citep{El-badry:2022} are possible in theory or $\sim\SI{167}{\minute}$ in practice \citep{Koen:2022}.

\section{Observations of contact binaries}

\alice

Observations of contact binaries date back to the 1940s \citep{Kuiper:1941}, and in the past eighty years the catalogue has grown to somewhere between $\sim\num{42000}$ \citep{Petrosky:2021} and $\sim\num{86000}$ \citep{Qian:2020} contact binaries, representing between 26.7 and 54.8 per cent of all observed binaries (those from the Washington Double Star catalogue; \citealt{Mason:2001}). The binaries we study in this chapter are summarised in \Cref{tab:overcontact_binaries}, and were originally compiled in \citet{Abdul-masih:2022}. Their sample focused on measuring the period stability of contact binaries and is thus subset of the known sample. They rejected a handful of other non O+O overcontact binaries, for reasons that include short-period von Zeipel-Kozai-Lidov oscillations, incomplete photometric / radial velocity fits, non-\gls{ms} binaries, unknown separations, and irregular light curves.

Ages for the six binaries have not been published, though a soft restriction can be placed on their evolutionary status; since \citet{Abdul-masih:2022} compiles specifically \gls{ms} contact binaries, their interactions must take place whilst the progenitor stars are on their respective main sequences. Nevertheless, we present inferred ages based on matches to the \gls{bpass} v2.2 models in \Cref{tab:hoki_matches}.

\subsection{Progenitor history}

We now examine each of the overcontact systems in detail, providing observational context to each system. For each binary, we list a selection of key measurements, including the fillout factor for each star. A fillout factor is a measurement of the degree to which a system overfills its Roche lobes, with $1 < f < 2$ denoting an overcontact binary, and a high fillout factor representing a more swelled and in-contact system.

\subsubsection{LSS 3074}

The binarity of LSS 3074 \citep{Raucq:2017} was first observed in 1985 by \citet{Morrell:1990} with an observed period of \SI{2.19}{\day}, with its HeI and HeII spectra causing the authors to conclude that it was an O4f+ and O6-7 binary, with inclination-weighted masses of $M_1\sin^3 i = \SI{8}{\msun}$ and $M_2\sin^3 i = \SI{9}{\msun}$. With later measurements of the inclinations to be $i\approx\ang{24}$, these masses were refined to $M_1 = \SI{17.2}{\msun}$ and $M_2=\SI{14.8}{\msun}$. It is located behind the Coalsack Nebula, at a distance of \SI{2256}{\pc}, with a B-band magnitude of \SI{12.81}{\mag} \citep{Stephenson:1971}. It has a fillout factor $f=1.05$.

\subsubsection{MY Camelopardalis}

MY Camelopardalis \citep[MY Cam;][]{Lorenzo:2014} is a binary in the cluster Alicante 1, and its binarity was established in 2003 by \citet{Negueruela:2003}. It is likely to be a very massive merger progenitor, though is barely in contact with $f=1.01$ \citep{Abdul-masih:2022}.

\subsubsection{SMC 108086}

OGLE SMC-SC10 108086 \citep[SMC 108086;][]{Hilditch:2005} is a O9 + O9.5/B0 binary. It is a system deeply in contact, with $f=1.70$ \citep{Hilditch:2005, Abdul-masih:2022} -- the deepest known -- and has a short period of $P=\SI{0.88}{\day}$. It is located at a distance of \SI{56.5}{\kilo\pc}, in the \gls{smc}.

\subsubsection{TU Muscae}

TU Muscae \citep[TU Mus;][]{Penny:2008} is an O8V + B0V binary discovered by \citet{Oosterhoff:1928, Oosterhoff:1930}. Its binarity was confirmed as late as 1971, in \citet{Knipe:1971}. The system was profiled in \citet{Andersen:1975}, who concluded that, based on comparison to theoretical models by \citet{Stothers:1972, Stothers:1973}, the system was of solar composition ($Z=0.021\approx\si{\zsun}$) and on the main sequence with $X_\text{H}=0.739$. It has a fillout factor of $f=1.12$.

\subsubsection{V382 Cygni}

V382 Cygni \citep[V382 Cyg;][]{Martins:2017} is an O6.5 + O6 binary whose components are oblate spheroids (deduced from the $\beta$-Lyrae light curves; \citealt{Pearce:1952}). It was originally discovered by \citet{Morgenroth:1935, Petrov:1946}. It has a fillout factor of $f=1.10$.

\subsubsection{VFTS 352}

VFTS 352 was detected in the VLT-FLAMES survey of the Tarantula Nebula \citep{Evans:2011} and is the most recent detection in our sample. The Tarantula Nebula / 30 Doradus is a large H(II) region in the \gls{lmc}. The progenitor of VFTS 352 is likely an equal mass, short period binary with no strong helium or nitrogen surface abundances \citep{Abdul-masih:2019}, which may be due to biases in the CNO abundances, or similar, in that atmospheric study. It has a fillout factor of $f=1.28$.

\subsection{Data matching with BPASS v2.2}\label{sec:BPASS_data_matching}

We perform our data matching using the \codename{Hoki} code \citep{Stevance:2020a}. The search parameters are shown in \Cref{tab:overcontact_binaries}. We query the \gls{bpass} v2.2.1 dataset and record whether or not they enter the (widened) parameter values. Parameters are widened as some of the periods are tighter than the \gls{bpass} bins and thus can be missed in the search.

The matching values \codename{Hoki} finds are shown in \Cref{tab:hoki_matches}. The primary models are also shown on \Cref{fig:hoki_matches_HRD_primaries}. We also report in the table the inferred ages at match, which lie between $\mu_{\log(\text{age})}=6.13$ (for MY Cam) and $\mu_{\log(\text{age})}=7.14$ (for TU Mus).

We note two key caveats in our search criteria. Our search error bars are widened, in some cases substantially. The known parameters in \citet{Abdul-masih:2022} are so tightly constrained that the parameter grid spacing in \gls{bpass} becomes significant, and thus the search bars must be widened to account for this.

Secondly, luminosities are not uniformly reported for the companions across the grid. In particular, the companion luminosity for VFTS 352 is unreported, and thus we approximate it using the Stefan-Boltzmann law,

\begin{equation}
    \frac{L}{L_\odot} = \left(\frac{R}{R_\odot}\right)^2 \left(\frac{T}{T_\odot}\right)^4,
\end{equation}

where $T_\odot=\SI{5778}{\kelvin}$. This induces two assumptions: that the companion is a perfect blackbody, and that it is spherical. The first assumption is typically assumed regardless, and the secondary is a necessary condition of our 1D stellar evolution approach. Although the star is, physically, distorted with a fillout factor of $f=1.28$, the key assumption underpinning 1D stellar evolution is spherical symmetry. Nevertheless, the illustration on the \gls{hrd} is for illustrative purposes only. Since the binaries in this sample are eclipsing binaries, the most confident parameters we could fit to are the masses and radii.

\section{Forward evolution}\label{sec:forward_evolution}

For each matched model, we extract the masses and periods, and then use these as input to our stellar modelling code. In particular, we use the modifications earlier described that enable helium-rich mass transfer.

In terms of periods, we examine the models at three periods. First is the fiducial period, as reported from the \codename{Hoki} code.
