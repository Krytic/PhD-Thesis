\chapter{Helium Rich Mass Transfer}\label{chap:he_mass_transfer}

\chapterEpigraph{Isn't it enough to see that a garden is beautiful without having to believe that there are fairies at the bottom of it too?}{Douglas Adams}

\lettrine[lines=3]{S}{ince the force between two} objects due to gravity scales like $F\sim\frac{1}{r_\text{sep}}$, another question naturally arises during the consideration of contact binaries. For sufficiently close binaries, one can imagine that mass transfer through \gls{rlof} could become reasonably strong, stripping the hydrogen envelope of a main sequence (or shortly post-main sequence) star and exposing the helium-enriched core underneath. If this stripping does not cause the star to retreat within its Roche lobe, then this core material would continue to be accreted, creating helium-enriched matter streams accreting onto its companion.

As discussed in \cref{sec:binary_evolution}, the presence of a companion measurably alters the evolution of a star. Typically this manifests as allowing matter to be exchanged between the components or the gravity of one star warping the photosphere of the other. In this chapter we focus on the first instance, where mass transfer occurs between two stars. In particular, we consider a binary system with \gls{zams} masses of \SI[parse-numbers=false]{30+10}{\msun}, and an initial period of $10^{0.6}\approx$ \SI{3.98}{\day}. We specifically explore this case as a case study on the effect of helium enriched mass transfer, and in \cref{chap:population_properties} we will explore the effect that this has on a grid of close binaries.

The general background for this section remains as elaborated in \cref{chap:theoretical_background}, however our parameterisation requires us to delinate between two cases: that when variable composition accretion is enabled and the case where it is disabled. These correspond to setting the parameter IVARACC in \cref{chap:param_config} to 1 or 0 respectively.

\subsection[Pre-interaction]{Pre-interaction (stages a-b)}\label{sec:pre_interaction_stages_a_b_}

The pre-interaction phase of both stars is identical in both cases, as the key change enabling this work only begins when \gls{rlof} occurs. In both cases, it evolves as a fully detached binary for \SI{4.51}{\mega\year}, with the donor almost completing its main sequence evolution. Hydrogen is exhausted in the core, and a helium core develops. In both cases, as the donor nears the Hertzsprung gap hook, it inflates to fill its Roche lobe, causing matter to begin to be exchanged via \gls{rlof} and kick-starting the pre-contact phase.

\subsection[Pre-contact]{Pre-contact (stages b-c)}\label{sec:pre_contact_stages_b_c_}

When the donor swells to fill its Roche lobe, it begins to efficiently transfer material to its companion. The increased drag causes the orbit to become hardened; shrinking by approximately 23 per cent. The accretor begins to accrete material, bleeding angular momentum into the system and causing the accretor to spin up. As the contact phase approaches, it approaches 40 per cent of the critical rotation rate, as shown in \cref{fig:stellar_profiles}. It reaches approximately 20 per cent when the contact phase begins.

\section{Atypical abundance profiles}

Because the stars are quite close together and have a mass ratio of $\nicefrac{1}{3}$, when \gls{rlof} starts it is quite voracious, and becomes even more so during the contact phase. The donor star sheds its hydrogen-rich surface and material from the helium-rich core is exposed, which too becomes part of the accreted material. The material stream becomes increasingly helium-rich, depositing this material on top of a hydrogen-rich surface, causing the accretor to have an atypical abundance profile, as shown pictorally in \cref{fig:composition_profile_evolution}. The net effect is that there is a helium-rich surface sitting atop a hydrogen-rich layer sitting atop a helium-rich core. At this stage thermohaline mixing is projected to occur, as there is an unstable composition gradient (owing to helium having a higher mean molecular weight than hydrogen).

\section{Comparison between variable and non-variable composition accretion}

In \cref{fig:hrd_comparison_var_novarr} we display a comparative HR diagram between the cases of variable and non-variable composition accretion. In each case an \SI[parse-numbers=false]{30+10}{\msun} binary was evolved with the only difference between the two being whether material was allowed to have variable composition accretion or the standard accretion prescription. In the bottom panel only the companion is shown, as the donor has identical evolution in both cases.

The first and most striking conclusion that we can draw from this result is that the companion is, in the variable composition accretion case, hotter and more luminous than the non-variable case.

\section{Implications}

\subsection{Spectroscopic masses}

Throughout the history of binary evolution theory, a consistent thread has been the consistent underprediction of the mass predicted by models (the ``evolutionary mass'') vs the mass inferred from spectroscopic studies of stars (the ``spectroscopic mass''). Summaries are found in works such as \citet{Herrero:1992, Massey:2009, Bouret:2013, Massey:2013, McEvoy:2015, Markova:2018, Ramachandran:2018, Bestenlehner:2020}. The reason has been left as either deficiencies in the physics of model atmospheres \citep{Herrero:1992} or non-included or not well-understood physics \citep{Bestenlehner:2020}.

This phenomenon is not unique to one code or methodology. To name but four, it has been observed in:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item BONNSAI (work in \citealt{Bestenlehner:2020}, BONNSAI in \citealt{Schneider:2014}),
    \item the work of \citet{Maeder:1990} \citep{Herrero:1992},
    \item the Geneva tracks (work by \citealt{Markova:2018}, Geneva code by \citealt{Ekstrom:2012}), and
    \item the PoWR code (work by \citealt{Ramachandran:2018}, PoWR code in \citealt{Grafener:2002, Hamann:2003, Sander:2015}).
\end{enumerate}

If luminosities are systematically incorrect, as discussed above, then there would be a corresponding systematic incorrect calculation of the mass; that is, a traditional mass-luminosity relationship may not hold. We show in \cref{fig:mass_luminosity_relationship} the relationship between the evolutionary mass and luminosity for both variable and non-variable composition accretion cases, as evolved in \autoref{sec:future_history}. We also display this for integer mass single-star models. Unsurprisingly, the non-variable accretion case evolves like a \SI{24}{\msun} model -- the initial mass of the model when this single-star evolution began was \SI{23.71}{\msun}, and the abundance profile typifies a single star; since the mass transfer was hydrogen-dominated, the profile is not inverted and thus resembles a single star.

On the other hand, the variable accretion case would (if observed) initially appear to be more massive than a \SI{24}{\msun} star (despite being \SI{23.66}{\msun} initially), eventually appearing to be approximately \SI{21}{\msun} -- i.e., $\sim$\SI{3}{\msun} less massive than it actually would be.

Consequently, any masses inferred from spectroscopy or luminosity measurements may be an underestimate of the true (evolutionary) mass. This would affect, for example, the inferred masses of compact objects and thus may have implications for our understanding of the progenitors of gravitational waves.
