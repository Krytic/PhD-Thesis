\section{Stellar Formation}\label{sec:stellar_formation}

\epigraph{Nature and nature's laws lay hid in night: God said, ``Let Newton be!'' and all was light.}{Alexander Pope}

From the point of view of stellar evolution, which starts in earnest at the \gls{zams}, the formation of stars is interesting but ultimately has little to no impact on the nuclear evolution of the star besides setting the initial mass. We provide a short recap of stellar formation here as scene-setting, but our stellar evolution models assume that a gas cloud has already collapsed and we have a star at its \gls{zams}.

Stars are born in clouds of gas and dust. Due to inhomegeneities in the dust formation, some regions become more dense than others. When a region of this gas with adiabatic constant $\gamma$ and mean molecular weight $\mu$ reaches the so-called Jeans Mass,

\begin{equation}
    M_\text{J} = \pqty{\frac{10k_\text{B}T}{3\pqty{\gamma-1}\mu m_\text{H}G}}^\frac32 \pqty{\frac{3}{4\pi\rho}}^\frac12 \sim \pqty{\frac{10k_\text{B}T}{G}R}^\frac32 \pqty{\frac{1}{M}}^\frac12,
\end{equation}

where the similarity is taking $\gamma=\frac53, \mu=\frac12$ for an adiabatic gas composed of H(II), then gravity becomes the dominant force in the clumps of gas. This causes a protostar to form. Generally speaking, this protostar slowly gains more mass from its surroundings \citep{Mckee:2007} and, as it does so, increases in temperature. When the core temperature reaches $\SI[parse-numbers=false]{10^7}{\kelvin}$, the hydrogen in the core begins to fuse into helium via the reaction chain

\begin{align*}
    \ce{^2_1H + ^2_1H &-> ^3_2He + ^1_0n + \SI{3.27}{\mega\electronvolt}} \\
    \ce{^2_1H + ^3_2He &-> ^4_2He + ^1_1H + \SI{18.3}{\mega\electronvolt}}.
\end{align*}

This is aided by two further hydrogen to helium reactions:

\begin{align*}
    \ce{^2_1H + ^2_1H &-> ^3_1He + ^1_1H + \SI{4.03}{\mega\electronvolt}} \\
    \ce{^2_1H + ^3_1H &-> ^4_2He + ^1_0n + \SI{17.59}{\mega\electronvolt}},
\end{align*}

or, via a master equation,

\begin{equation}
    \ce{6^2_1H -> 2^4_2He + 2^1_1H + 2^1_0n + \SI{43.2}{\mega\electronvolt}},
\end{equation}

with intermediate products which remain stable and are thus omitted for simplicity. The burning of $^{2}_{1}$H -- deuterium -- occurs for a short fraction of the lifetime of the star, before core hydrogen burning begins in earnest. This energy release will sustain the star throughout its lifetime, providing outwards pressure resisting gravity pulling the star inwards, in a balance termed \textit{hydrostatic equilibrium}. Once hydrogen fusion has begun, we say that the star has reached \gls{zams}, and is now on the \textit{main sequence}, where it will spend approximately 90\% of its lifetime.

\section{Stellar Evolution}

\mediaepigraph{It did not last: the Devil howling ``Ho! Let Einstein be!'' restored the status quo.}{Answer to Pope's Epitaph for Sir Isaac Newton}{J. C. Squire}

Now that our protostar has evolved onto the main sequence, it has become a star in its own right. Over the next few million years, it will continue to burn hydrogen into helium, forming a helium core. It is at this point that the evolution encounters a number of bifurcation points, depending on the mass and chemical composition of the star. In general, while the star lives, the four equations of stellar structure hold,

\begin{equation}
    \left.\begin{array}{lll}
        \dv{m}{r} &= 4\pi r^2\rho & \text{(mass continuity)}, \\
        \dv{p}{r} &= -\frac{GM\rho}{r^2} & \text{(hydrostatic equilibrium)},\\
        \dv{T}{r} &= -\frac{3\kappa\rho L}{64\pi r^2\sigma T^3} & \text{(energy transport), and} \\
        \dv{L}{r} &= 4\pi r^2\rho\epsilon & \text{(thermal equilibrium)},
    \end{array}\right\}
    \label{eq:stellar_structure}
\end{equation}

where $\epsilon$ is the rate of energy energation per unit mass, $\kappa$ is the opacity of the material, $\sigma$ is the Stefann-Boltzmann constant, $m$ is the mass internal to radius $r$, $p$ is pressure, $\rho$ is density, $M$ is total mass of the star, and $L$ is the luminosity.

In addition to \cref{eq:stellar_structure}, an \gls{eos} demanding $\rho=\rho(P, T)$ is desired. We take our \gls{eos} from \citet{Pols:1995}, which built upon that in \citet{Eggleton:1973}. Critically, Pols' \gls{eos} includes the dissociation of molecular hydrogen, Coulomb interactions, and pressure ionization, which \citet{Eggleton:1973} lacked. The \gls{eos} is an approximation to Fermi-Dirac statistics, and originates from the free energy

\begin{equation}
    F(V, T, N_\text{e}, N_\text{e}) = -\frac13 aT^4 V + kT\sum_i N_\text{i} \pqty{\ln\frac{N_\text{i} h^3}{\omega_i V\pqty{2\pi m_i kT}^\frac32}-1+\frac{\chi_i}{kT}}+F_\text{e}(N_\text{e}, V, T)+\Delta F_\text{e}(N_\text{e}, V, T)
\end{equation}

These equations represent conservation of mass, hydrostatic equilibrium, energy transport, and thermal equilibrium, respectively. Knowing constants $\epsilon, \kappa$, we have four equations with four unknowns. It is usually convenient to use the \textit{Rosseland mean} opacity,

\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{\kappa} = \frac{\pi}{acT^3}\int_0^\infty \frac{1}{\kappa_\nu}\pdv{B}{T}~\mathrm{d}\nu
\end{equation}

where $a$ is the radiation density constant ($a=\SI{7.57e-15}{\erg\per\cubic\cm\per\quartic\kelvin}$) and $B$ is the black-body intensity at frequency $\nu$ and temperature $T$. The function $\kappa_\nu$ is obtainable from atom-electron-photon interaction \citep{Eldridge:2004a}: bound-bound, bound-free, free-free (bremsstrahlung) transitions, and electron scattering. $\kappa_\nu$ is thus related to the scattering cross-section $\sigma_\nu = \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\pi \dv{\sigma}{\Omega}\sin\theta\, \mathrm{d}{\theta}\, \mathrm{d}{\phi}$. The Rosseland mean becomes

\begin{equation}
    \kappa = \frac{T^5 a c^3 k_\text{B}}{\pi h^2 \int_0^\infty \frac{\nu^4}{\left(\cosh{\left(\frac{h \nu}{T k_\text{B}} \right)} - 1\right) \kappa_\nu}\, d\nu},
\end{equation}

though the integral cannot be analytically solved further. On the other hand, $\epsilon$ is obtainable from knowing the nuclear reactions occurring in the star's core, namely

\begin{equation}
    \epsilon = \sum_{i}^N \text{energy output of reaction } i.
\end{equation}

The reaction rates and energy outputs are typically inferred from empirical data; we take the interpolation in temperature from \citet{Caughlan:1988}, and correct the rates to approximate the cross-sections of \citet{Bahcall:1992, Bahcall:1995}.

All told, in the simplest sense, the internal \textit{structure} of the star can be modelled by hand by solving \cref{eq:stellar_structure} from $m=0$ to $m=M$.

\subsection{The chemical composition of stars}

\alice

A star contains at its core a complex nuclear furnace. Since stars are born in hydrogen-rich regions of space and obtain high enough pressures in the core to ignite fusion, the star will be sustained by the burning of predominantly hydrogen and helium. We thus group the elements into hydrogen, helium, and metals (which we define as anything with atomic number $\geq3$, i.e., lithium and heavier elements). It is convenient to speak of the mass fraction of the element, i.e., the fraction by mass of a star that is a certain element. For our purposes, this is expressible as

\begin{equation}
	X+Y+\sum_{i=0}^{115} \mathcal{Z}_i = 1, \label{eq:mass_fracs}
\end{equation}

where $X$ is the mass fraction of hydrogen, $Y$ of helium, and $\mathcal{Z}_i$ as the metal\footnote{It is custom in astronomy to refer to anything with an atomic number greater than 2 as a metal, rather than the conventional definition of a material conductive at absolute zero, including most of the d-block, s-block, f-block, and about half of the p-block on the periodic table.} with atomic number $i+3$. \JanNote{What about isotopes?} When speaking about the chemical composition of a star it is also convenient to speak of the \textit{metallicity}, the fraction at \gls{zams} of the star that is metal. For this, we define the metallicity as

\begin{equation}
    Z \equiv \sum_{i=0}^{115} \mathcal{Z}_i,
\end{equation}

such that \cref{eq:mass_fracs} becomes

\begin{equation}
    X+Y+Z = 1.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Evolutionary Pathways}

In this section, we examine the three major regimes of stellar evolution for single-stars: low-mass (\cref{sec:low_mass_stars}), intermediate-mass (\cref{sec:intermediate_mass_stars}), and massive stars (\cref{sec:massive_stars}). We do not consider stars about $M_\text{ZAMS}\approx\SI{80}{\msun}$ in this thesis \todo{why?}

\subsubsection{Low-Mass Stars: \texorpdfstring{$\SI{0.08}{\msun} < M < \SI{2}{\msun}$}{Stars with between 0.08 and 2 times the mass of the sun}}\label{sec:low_mass_stars}

Stars below $\approx\SI{0.5}{\msun}$ do not ignite helium, and become helium \glspl{wd}. Above this, they ignite helium degenerately in their cores, leading to thermal runaway. The star can peak at a luminosity close to $10^{11} L_\odot$ (approximately 100 times the peak luminosity of SN 1987A, per Figure 5 of \citet{Arnett:1989}). However, this peak in luminosity is attributable to nuclear fusion in the core, and the energy does not reach the surface, let alone escape into the \gls{ism} -- rather, it is absorbed into the outer layers of the star. During this extremely brief thermal runaway, the luminosity begins to settle and the star is able to continue evolving. Models of low mass stars fail at this stage and care is required to evolve them. This phenomena is called the \textit{helium flash} and is discussed further in \cref{sec:numerical_instabilities}.

After the ignition of helium, these stars cross the so-called Hertzsprung Gap -- the region where very few stars are observed empirically. This region is bounded (on a colour-magnitude diagram, which is a transform of the HR diagram) by the points $\pqty{M_v, B-V}=\left\{\pqty{2,0.5}, \pqty{-7, 0.1}, \pqty{-6, 1.6}\right\}$ \citep{Hoyle:1960}, where $M_v$ is the V-band magnitude (magnitude in the visual band), and $B-V$ is the colour index of the star. The phenomenon of a gap existing is attributable to the fact that stars cross this gap exceedingly quickly: whereas it spends $\sim90\%$ of its life on the main sequence, it spends $\sim2\%$ of its life crossing the Hertzsprung gap \citep{Hoyle:1960}.

% \footnote{The relationship between a colour-magnitude diagram and the HR diagram is that $\log L=0.4\pqty{M_{\text{bol},\odot}-M_{\text{bol},*}}\simeq0.4\pqty{4.74-M_{v,*}-\text{BC}}$, $\log T=\log\pqty{4600\pqty{\frac{1}{0.92\pqty{B-V}+1.7}+\frac{1}{0.92\pqty{B-V}+0.62}}}$, where BC is a bolometric correction which depends on the stellar type. This expression for the temperature is a fit provided by \citet{Ballesteros:2012}.}

\subsection{Stellar Atmospheres}

As a final note in the evolution of a single star, we must briefly address what occurs about the photosphere of the star. A star is not a planet -- there is no firm, opaque boundary demarcating the inner of a star from the outer. The common definition of the ``surface'' of a star is the surface of last scattering (equivalently, the photosphere), which is the point radially in which the mean free path $\ell$ of a photon emitted from the core of the star becomes meaningfully long (internally, $\ell_\gamma\sim\SI{2}{\cm}$, externally, $\ell_\gamma\sim\SI{4.4}{\pc}$).\footnote{The mean free path is a function of the particle under consideration: for a neutrino, internally $\ell_\nu\sim\SI{32}{\pc}$ and externally $\ell_\nu\sim\SI{81}{\ronna\pc}\sim 3\times10^{18}$ times the diameter of the observable Universe.} However, outflows from the star as well as the potential interaction of a partner in a common-envelope event mean that this surface is not necessarily firm: there is no sharp turn from stellar material to empty space. Thus, the simulation of stellar atmospheres is of interest to the stellar astrophysicist.

However, this introduces another rather large layer of complexity. The synthesis of stellar spectra is not a trivial task, even without considering the complexity of evolving the star simultaneously. \citet{Groh:2014} is one of the very few instances of coupled spectroscopic calculations with evolutionary calculations, which only evolved a single star. Moreover, this thesis is concerned with detailed modelling of the stellar \textit{interior}, and thus the atmosphere is an interesting sidenote, but one we shall not pursue.

A star's lifetime is not infinite. The key assumption underpinning stellar evolution is that of hydrostatic equilibrium: the counterplay of gravity against outwards pressure. In short, a dyanmically stable, non-rotating star composed of an ideal gas with adiabatic constant $\gamma$ and gravitational potential energy $\Omega$ radiates energy via

\begin{equation}
    L = -\dot{E} = -\frac{3\gamma-4}{3\pqty{\gamma-1}} \dv{\Omega}{t}-\dv{\gamma}{t}\pqty{\frac{1}{\pqty{\gamma-1}^2}},
\end{equation}

If the chemical gradient is largely unchanged, $\dv{\gamma}{t}\sim0$ so the luminosity reduces to

\begin{equation}
L = -\frac{3\gamma-4}{3\pqty{\gamma-1}} \dv{\Omega}{t}\quad\text{where}\quad U=-\frac{1}{3\pqty{\gamma-1}} \Omega,
\end{equation}

where $U$ is the total internal energy of the star. Since $\dot{E}<0$ for a star radiating energy, and $\Omega<0$ by definition (since assembling a star is energetically favourable), we have that $\dot{\Omega}<0$ and $\dot{U}>0$, i.e., that as the star radiates energy it attempts to shrink. This shrinking is arrested by the nuclear fusion in the core providing outwards pressure, meaning the star is stable and resists explosion. As the star continues to burn through its fuel supply and eventually exhaust it, gravity will win as the dominant force, and result in the star imploding in a \gls{ccsne}. From a thermodynamic perspective, as the star evolves, it shrinks as energy is bled off into the interstellar medium. This shrinkage compresses and heats the core, meaning the star's specific heat capacity is negative. This system is thermodynamically unstable, and thus every star either collapses into a remnant (cf. \gls{bh}, \gls{ns}) or is sustained in some form by electron degeneracy pressure (cf. \gls{wd}).

In any case, the breaking of hydrostatic equilibrium results generally results in\footnote{The extent of the veracity of this statement is an area of active research: \citet{Reynolds:2015} presents a short summary.} a supernova, which can be classified into subtypes, as shown in \cref{fig:supernova_taxonomy}. In this event, gravity pulls the stellar core inwards, before ``bouncing'' and ejecting the envelope into the interstellar medium. The physics of this approximately second-long event is highly uncertain, and is in general unable to be modelled by detailed stellar evolution codes. All models we generate -- assuming they have not been terminated earlier -- will terminate at the moment of supernova.

\subsection{Type Ia supernovae}

There is another class of supernovae we consider in this thesis, namely type Ia supernovae. These are formed when a \gls{wd} star exceeds the maximum mass by which electron degeneracy pressure can sustain the star. This mass is termed the Chandrasekhar mass, $M_\text{ch}\approx\SI{1.44}{\msun}$, and is derived in \cref{chap:additional_derivations}. When material accretes onto a white dwarf, its mass will increase, and if the accretion does not stop, the mass will exceed the Chandrasekhar mass. This means electron degeneracy pressure is no longer able to sustain the star, and the core collapses further into a Type Ia (or thermonuclear) supernova.

Introducing a second star into the mix complicates the evolution. \subsection{Binary star kinematics}\label{sec:binary_kinematics}

There are several formation channels for a binary star system. Binaries may be formed from birth (as discussed in \cref{sec:stellar_formation}, with the caveat that in this scenario two clumps of gas form near each other), via fragmentation of a protostar's disc, dynamical capture of one star by another (comparable to one of the suggested origins of Phobos and Deimos, per \citet{Burns:1978}), or via a higher multiplicity system ejecting one or more of its constituent stars, perhaps via a natal supernova kick.

In any case, once formed and the orbit is stabilised, binary systems obey Kepler's three laws. In the generalised binary regime, however, the assumption that $M_1\gg M_2$ (which underpins most statements of Kepler's laws) is not necessarily true -- for instance, the system LSS 3074 has two stellar components with \gls{zams} masses of approximately \SI{30}{\msun} and \SI{10}{\msun} \citep{Raucq:2017}. Moreover, the fact that there are two stars in orbit rather that one object orbiting another means that Kepler's laws must be stated more precisely. Formally, they can be expressed as (see the appendix of \citet{Richards:2021} for derivations):

\begin{enumerate}
    \item All orbits are ellipses, with both stars orbiting a common barycentre at the focus of the ellipse,
    \item A line segment joining the two stars sweeps out equal areas in equal times,
    \item The square of the period of the system is proportional to the cube of the semimajor axis of the ellipse (the binary separation), with the proportionality term dependent on the sum of the masses.
\end{enumerate}

The third law can be stated more precisely as

\begin{equation}
    T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{G(M_1+M_2)}a^3,
\end{equation}

where $T$ is the period of the binary, $M_1$ and $M_2$ the constituent masses, $G=\SI{6.67e-11}{\meter\cubed\per\kilogram\per\second\squared}$ is the universal gravitational constant, and $a$ is the semimajor axis of the binary. A schematic of the orbit of a binary system is given in \cref{fig:bss_schematic}.

\subsection{Roche Lobes}\label{sec:roche_lobes}

A binary system is complicated by the presence of its companions in several different ways. As alluded to in the previous section, the presence of a companion warps space-time in such a way that material can be quite distorted from a sphere or oblate spheroid but still remain gravitationally bound to the system. In the extremal case, a star can grow to fill or even exceed its Roche Lobe, which is the tear-drop shaped equipotential surface within which material remains gravitationally bound to its star. In \cref{fig:roche_potentials} we display these equipotential surfaces for a range of different mass ratios. It is pertinent to note that the shape of all of the Roche Lobes change rapidly at low mass rations, however at higher mass ratios the shapes change less rapidly. In an equal-mass ratio binary, the binary configuration has rotational symmetry about the $\mathcal{L}_1$ point, and reflectional symmetry about the $x$-axis and the line $y=\mathcal{L}_1$.

There are two primary Roche Lobe points we consider: the $\mathcal{L}_1$ point and the $\mathcal{L}_2$ point. The $\mathcal{L}_1$ point (also known as the inner Lagrangian point) is the nexus between the two stars, the $\mathcal{L}_2$ point (the outer Lagrangian point) is located on the other side of the secondary to the inner point. The dimensionless $x$-ordinate of the $\mathcal{L}_1$ point, $\xi$, measured in terms of the separation $a$, is given as the solution to

\begin{equation}
    0=\xi-\frac{\xi_2}{\pqty{\xi-\xi_1}^2}-\frac{\xi_1}{\pqty{\xi-\xi_2}^2},\label{eq:roche_lobe_condition}
\end{equation}

where $\xi_1=\frac{M_2}{M_1+M_2}$, and $\xi_2=\frac{M_1}{M_1+M_2}$.

\subsection{Mass Transfer}\label{sec:mass_transfer}

\alice

When two stars are sufficiently close in proximity, surface and near-surface events can cause accretion of material from one star to another due to the primary's stellar winds (whereas in the single-star case, this would be matter lost to the \gls{ism}). The nature of mass transfer is a complicated affair; most binary star population synthesis codes typically inherit their mass loss prescriptions from papers such as \citet{Bondi:1944, Hurley:2002}, and this approach is what we detail in this section.

Mass transfer can broadly be sectioned into four main categories:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item $\dot{M}_\text{wind}$, where matter is lost through stellar winds,
    \item $\dot{M}_\text{RLOF}$, where matter is transferred through one (or more) of a binary's Lagrange points (cf. \cref{sec:roche_lobes}),
    \item $\dot{M}_\text{acc}$, where matter is transferred through accretion (or interaction with a disk), and
    \item $\dot{M}_\text{Bondi}$, where matter proceeds through a \citet{Bondi:1944} type mechanism.
\end{enumerate}

Our mass loss rate for each star is constructed from these four terms as

\begin{equation}
    \dot{M} = \dot{M}_{\text{wind}} - \dot{M}_{\text{Bondi}} + \dot{M}_{\text{RLOF}} - \dot{M}_{\text{acc}}.\label{eq:mass_loss_full}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Wind mass-loss}

Wind mass loss computations are (generally) performed using a mix of theoretical and empiricial mass-loss rate prescriptions of \cite{De_jager:1988} and \citet{Vink:2001}. The precise scheme is detailed in \citet{Eldridge:2017}.\footnote{In other codes such as \textsc{Mesa}, this is referred to as the ``Dutch'' mass loss prescription owing to the nationality of the authors of \citet{De_jager:1988, Nugis:2000a, Vink:2001}.} There are two critical modifications we make to their scheme.

The first modification surrounds the transition into the \gls{wr} regime. Traditionally this has been a sharp transition when the surface hydrogen mass fraction becomes less than 0.4 and the surface temperature is above \SI[print-unity-mantissa=false]{1e4}{\kelvin}. This is a reasonable approximation, though if a star reaches this phase and is \textit{not} yet a \gls{wr} star, the code will fail. To account for this we gradually turn on these enhanced \gls{wr} rates when this condition approaches, smoothing the transition.

The second modification we have made is that if a star has a hydrogen exhausted core and begins approaching the \gls{wr} temperature range, we assume that \gls{wr} mass loss must begin and thus turn on the slowly-turning on \gls{wr} regime.

Mathematically, the computation for $\dot{M}_\text{wind}$ is more involved, though follows the same essential structure as \citet{Vink:2001}. Details are provided in \cref{chap:mass_loss_construction}.

\subsubsection{\acrfull{rlof}}

% \mediaepigraph{Your [roeche loebe overflaew] partner deserves better traetment!}{Baron from the Baronies}{Dimension 20: Fantasy High: Sophomore Year}

Matter extruded from the Lagrangian points is assumed to transfer with a mass loss rate similar to \citet{Hurley:2002}. We take

\begin{equation}
    \dot{M}_\text{RLOF} = \min\left(k\mathcal{R}\left(\frac{R}{R_\text{RL}}\right)^3,10^{-2}\right) \unit{\msun\per\year}.
\end{equation}

Here, $k$ (referred to in our code as RMT) parameterises effectively how much of the star we permit to exist outside of its Roche lobe before it is accreted -- i.e., the voracity of \gls{rlof}. The choice of this can be somewhat arbitrary. \citet{Hurley:2002} for example took $k=\num{3e-6}\min\left\{M_1, 5\right\}^2$, where the coefficient was ``chosen by experiment to ensure that the mass transfer is steady'', and warned that for degenerate stars a more appropriate choice would be to increase this by a factor of $10^3$. Instead, we choose to adopt $k=10^3$ outright with no dependence on the mass, finding instead that this replicated other work -- allowing efficient \gls{rlof} -- best.

Matter exceeding $\mathcal{L}_2$ (the outer Lagrange point) does not currently receive special treatment. The implications of $\mathcal{L}_2$ overflow are significant. This ejection will lead to unstable mass transfer, shrinking the orbit and likely inducing a \gls{cee} event \citep{Fragos:2023}. There is debate about the appropriate treatment here. \citet{Linial:2017} indexes the $\mathcal{L}_2$ rate to the $\mathcal{L}_1$ rate via an efficiency parameter. \citet{Li:2013} considers the case analogous to $\mathcal{L}_1$ outflow, where material outflowing from $\mathcal{L}_2$ acts to retard the angular momentum. In their notation $\dv{J}{t}=-\epsilon\dot{M}_1 a_{L2}^2\omega$ (compare $\dv{J}{t}=-\beta\dot{M}_1 a_{L1}^2\omega$ for \gls{rlof} through $\mathcal{L}_1$) for controllable parameters $\beta$ and $\epsilon$. The ambiguity of treatment leads to some codes to terminate at the onset of $\mathcal{L}_2$ outflow, such as \codename{Posydon} \citep{Fragos:2023}. $\mathcal{L}_2$ outflow requires stars to be in reasonably tight orbits ($\sim\SI{2}{\day})$, which we will approach, but none of our stars will enter this regime, and thus we do not afford it special treatment.

\subsubsection{Accretion}

Our accretion rates are taken from \citet{Bondi:1944, Hurley:2002}, and limits the accretion rate by the thermal timescale of the star. The prescription is that

\begin{equation}
    \dot{M}_\text{acc} = \min\left(\dot{M}_\text{RLOF}, \frac{M}{T_\text{KH}}\mathcal{R}\left(1-\frac{\omega}{0.8\omega_\text{crit}}\right)\right),
\end{equation}

where $\omega$ and $\omega_\text{crit}$ are the spin rate and critical spin rate of the star. We generally assume that the star is composed of individually rotating shells of variable thickness. If $N$ is the number of meshpoints that the star is composed of (for our cases, normally $N=199$), then the moment of inertia is given by

\begin{equation}
    I = \frac25 M_\text{c}R_\text{c}^2 + \sum_{k=1}^N \frac23 \left(m_k-m_{k+1}\right)r_k^2,
\end{equation}

where $m_k$ is the mass internal to meshpoint $k$ (sim. for $r_k$), and a subscript of c refers to the central meshpoint. It is assumed that the central meshpoint sits atop a solid sphere, hence $\frac25$ as its prefactor.

\subsubsection{Bondi-Hoyle accretion}

Bondi-Hoyle accretion for a circular orbit is given by

\begin{equation}
\dot{M}_\text{Bondi} = \min\left[-\left(\frac{GM_2}{v_\text{W}^2}\right)^2 \frac{\alpha_\text{W}}{2a^2}\frac{1}{\left(1+v^2\right)^{3/2}}, 0.8\right]\dot{M}_\text{wind},\label{eq:bondi}
\end{equation}

where we have assumed that the orbital velocity is given by

\begin{equation}
    v^2 = \frac{G\left(M_1+M_2\right)}{a v_\text{W}^2},
\end{equation}

and that the wind velocity is given by

\begin{equation}
    v_\text{W} = 2\beta_\text{W}\frac{GM_1}{R_1}.
\end{equation}

Generalising this to an eccentric orbit causes the first argument in \cref{eq:bondi} to pick up a coefficient of $\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-e^2}}$. There are two free parameters to select: $\alpha_\text{W}$ and $\beta_\text{W}$. These ostensibly depend on stellar types, though we select for simplicity $\alpha_\text{W}=\frac32$ \citep{Boffin:1988} and $\beta_\text{W}=7$ \citep{Lamers:1995} for O-type stars. This parameterisation is likely incomplete; \citet{Hurley:2002} argues that as per \citet{Lamers:1995}, $\beta_\text{W}$ should vary between 7 for O-type stars to 0.5 for A and F-type stars, perhaps descending as low as $\beta_\text{W}\approx\frac18$ for cool supergiants. We leave this implementation for future work under the assumption that our mass-loss rate is correct to within an order of magnitude. % concern.jpeg

\section{Post-Supernova}\label{sec:post_SN}

\mediaepigraph{Wit nodded.\\
``Oh, is this the place where I talk?'' Kaladin said.\\
``If you wish.''\\
``I don’t wish. Get on with the story.''}{Rhythm of War}{Brandon Sanderson}

After a supernova, the star leaves behind its core as a compact remnant. Generally, these are either white dwarves, black holes, or neutron stars. The interior structure of each is either complicated or, in the case of black holes, impossible to observationally probe without violating causality. Their orbital motions are now deterministic from four initial parameters: $m_1, m_2, a$, and $e$. Motion is now wholly determined by the emission of gravitational radiation through (detectable) gravitational waves. The pertinent equations of motion are given by \citet{Peters:1964}, namely,

\begin{align}
    \dv{a}{t} &= \frac{-\beta}{a^3\left(1-e^2\right)^\frac72}\left(1+\frac{73}{24}e^2+\frac{37}{96}e^4\right),\text{ and} \label{eq:dadt}\\
    \dv{e}{t} &= -\frac{19}{12}\frac{\beta}{a^4\left(1-e^2\right)^\frac52}\left(e+\frac{121}{304}e^3\right),\label{eq:dedt}
\end{align}

with the constant $\beta$ defined as

\begin{equation}
    \beta \equiv \frac{64}{5}\frac{G^3mM\pqty{m+M}}{c^5}.
\end{equation}

Now if one defines the constant $c_0$ through

\begin{equation}
    c_0 = a_0\pqty{1-e_0^2}e_0^{-\frac{12}{19}}\pqty{1+\frac{121}{304}e_0^2}^{-\frac{870}{2299}},
\end{equation}

then the time from the second supernova to the conclusion of the inspiral, termed the \textit{coalescence time}, will be given by

\begin{equation}
    T_\text{c}(a_0, e_0) = \frac{12}{19}\frac{c_0^4}{\beta}\int_0^{e_0} \frac{e^\frac{22}{19}\left(1+\frac{121}{304}e^2\right)^\frac{1181}{2299}}{\left(1-e^2\right)^\frac32}{ \mathrm{d}e}.\label{eq:coalescence_time}
\end{equation}

The derivations for \cref{eq:dadt,eq:dedt,eq:coalescence_time} are given in \cref{chap:additional_derivations}. The integral has no closed-form solution, and a numerical integrator is typically used to compute the coalescence time. However, some approximations such as \citet{Mandel:2021a} do exist and approximate the coalescence time rather well over the entire range of mass ratios.

\subsection{Merger Rates of compact binaries}

The merger rate of a population of compact binaries at time $t$ after formation can be formalised via the heuristic

\begin{equation}
    m(t) = \text{number of binaries for which $T_\text{c} + T_\text{rej} + T_\text{evo} < t$},\label{eq:definition_of_merger_rate}
\end{equation}

where $T_\text{c}$ is the coalescence time, $T_\text{rej}$ is the rejuvenation age (the age at which the primary star last accreted mass), and $T_\text{evo}$ is the evolution time-scale (the length of time the primary spends on the main sequence). $m(t)$ is called the \gls{dtd} of a population.

However, the underlying assumption in this is that the population was formed after an instantaneous burst of star-forming activity at time $t=0$, which is clearly unphysical. We must account for the so-called \gls{sfrd} $\psi(z)$, assumed to be taken from \citet{Madau:2014}:

\begin{equation}
    \psi(z) = 0.015\frac{(1+z)^{2.7}}{1+\left(\frac{1+z}{2.9}\right)^{5.6}}~\unit{\msun\per\year\per\mega\pc\cubed},
\end{equation}

where $z$ is the redshift. For the purposes of converting between lookback time $t$ and redshift $z$, we assume a standard $\Lambda$CDM cosmology with $\Omega_\text{M}=0.3, \Omega_\Lambda=0.7, \Omega_k = 0, H_0=\SI{70}{\km\per\second\per\mega\pc}$.

Of course, this ignores the effect of mixed-metallicity models, which we account for using the presciption of \citet{Langer:2006}, whereupon the \gls{sfrd} is multiplied by a factor $\chi$:

\begin{equation}
    \chi\left(z,Z\right) = \frac{\hat{\Gamma}\left[0.84, \left(\frac{Z}{Z_\odot}\right)^2\cdot 10^{0.3z}\right]}{\Gamma\left(0.84\right)},
\end{equation}

where $\hat{\Gamma}$ and $\Gamma$ are the upper incomplete and complete Gamma functions respectively. This means our \gls{sfh} -- which takes into account both redshift and metallicity -- denoted $\Psi(z,Z)$, is given by

\begin{equation}
    \Psi(z,Z) = 0.015\int_{z_1}^{z_2} \frac{(1+z)^{2.7}}{1+\left(\frac{1+z}{2.9}\right)^{5.6}}\frac{\hat{\Gamma}\left[0.84, \left(\frac{Z}{Z_\odot}\right)^2\cdot 10^{0.3z}\right]}{\Gamma\left(0.84\right)}~\mathrm{d}z,
\end{equation}

where $z_1$ and $z_2$ are the redshifts of our bin edges. The event rate as a function of time, $\mathcal{R}(t)$, is therefore given by

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{R}(t) = \Psi(z_t) * m(t),\label{eq:event_rate_distribution} %= \int_0^\infty m(z_t) \cdot 0.015\int_{z_1}^{z_2} \frac{(1+z-z')^{2.7}}{1+\left(\frac{1+z-z'}{2.9}\right)^{5.6}}\frac{\hat{\Gamma}\left[0.84, \left(\frac{Z}{Z_\odot}\right)^2\cdot 10^{0.3(z-z')}\right]}{\Gamma\left(0.84\right)}~\mathrm{d}z~\mathrm{d}z',
\end{equation}

where $z_t$ is the redshift corresponding to lookback time $t$, given by the solution to the equation

\begin{equation}
    t = t_\text{H} \int_0^{z_t} \frac{\dd{z^\prime}}{\pqty{1+z^\prime}\sqrt{\Omega_\text{M}\pqty{1+z^\prime}^3+\Omega_\text{k}\pqty{1+z^\prime}^2+\Omega_\Lambda}},
\end{equation}

with $t_\text{H}$ the Hubble time, and $\Omega_\text{M}, \Omega_\text{k}, \Omega_\Lambda$ the matter density, curvature parameter, and cosmological constant density parameter of the Universe. In general this integral does not have a solution, however in the case of $\Lambda$CDM with Hubble constant $H_0=\SI{70}{\km\per\second\per\mega\pc}$ as outlined above, an exact solution for redshift is

\begin{equation}
z = \pqty{\frac{a}{\sinh\pqty{\frac{b \operatorname{asinh}{\left(a \right)} + t}{b}}}}^\frac23 - 1
\end{equation}

where $a = \sqrt{\frac73}, b = \frac{1}{105\sqrt{0.7}}$.\footnote{In general in a flat Universe (with $\Omega_\text{k}=0$), $a=\sqrt{\frac{\Omega_\Lambda}{\Omega_\text{M}}}$, and $b=\frac{1}{H_0}\frac{2}{3\sqrt{\Omega_\text{M}}}$. This integral was computed via \textsc{Sympy}.} Detail on how the convolution in \cref{eq:event_rate_distribution} is computed may be found in \citet{Richards:2021}.

\subsubsection{Brief History}

\mediaepigraph{And through the night, behind the wheel, the mileage clicking west\\I think upon Mackenzie, David Thompson and the rest\\Who cracked the mountain ramparts and did show a path for me\\To race the roaring Fraser to the sea.}{Northwest Passage}{Stan Rogers}

Stellar modelling became a field of active research in the 1950s. Prior to the invention of the computer, a model was treated as a mathematical process and thus was computed -- often labouriously -- by hand. For instance, in \citet{Eggleton:1967}, Eggleton writes that

\begin{fanciful}
    Since we have seen in Section 3 that the structure of the flame zone can be obtained independently (almost) of the rest of the star, it is reasonable to expect that instabilities of the type considered by Schwarzchild \& H\"arm can also be examined in this way, so that the considerable labour of computing the structure of an entire star can be avoided.
    \begin{flushright}
        -- \citet{Eggleton:1967}
    \end{flushright}
\end{fanciful}

The Cambridge \codename{stars} code was one of the first detailed stellar evolution codes to be written, though initially it only modelled one star in detail. To model a binary system, tweaks were made whereupon the primary would be modelled in detail whilst approximating the secondary with the equations of \citet{Hurley:2002} until the primary supernova, at which point the secondary would be evolved in detail. This had its merits; notably computation time was saved, and the results were generally accurate anyway.

However, \citet{Stancliffe:2009} introduced detailed modelling of \textit{both} stars in a binary system into the \codename{stars} code -- that is, the interiors of both stars were computed in detail, alongside the simualtaneous computation of the orbital parameters. Since then, a wide array of improvements have been made to the numerical stability of the code, as well as introducing or refining new physics.

There are four variants of this code:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The Cambridge \codename{stars} code, which is the version originally developed by Peter Eggleton and originally described in \citet{Eggleton:1971}. This version was originally only capable of solving the equations for a single star.
    \item The \codename{Eldridge stars} code described in \citet{Eldridge:2004, Eldridge:2004a, Eldridge:2017}, which is the version used to generate the fiducial \gls{bpass} v2 grids. This version models the primary in detail while approximating the secondary using the equations of \citet{Hurley:2002}.
    \item The \codename{bstars} code, which is the unmodified version lately available from Cambridge, described in \citet{Stancliffe:2009}. This version models both stars in detail throughout their lives.
    \item The \codename{Aotearoa stars} code, which is the version of the code incorporating the changes we have made.
\end{enumerate}

Unless context otherwise requires, when we refer to ``the \codename{stars} code'' with no further qualification, we are referring specifically to the \codename{Aotearoa stars} code.

\subsubsection{Matrix Inversion}

The heart of \codename{stars} is a matrix inversion algorithm. Although computing the inverse of a matrix is a polynomial time process, it can still take considerable time to invert. \codename{stars} implements a Henyey matrix \citep{Henyey:1964}, which is of size $n=(4+n_\text{comp})N_\text{mesh}$, with $n_\text{comp}$ the number of equations to be solved and $N_\text{mesh}$ the number of meshpoints in the model. An individual element of the Henyey matrix is given by

\begin{equation}
    \mat{H} = \pdv{\pqty{\text{equation } i}}{\pqty{\text{variable } j}}.
\end{equation}

For a ``typical'' binary computation, with $n_\text{comp}=30$ equations and $N_\text{mesh}=199$ meshpoints, the matrix is already $6766\times6766$, for a binary which evolves up the \gls{agb} (the theoretically most complex calculation we perform) with $N_\text{mesh}=999$, the matrix is $33966\times33966$. Introducing $k$ new equations into the code causes the runtime of the matrix inversion calculation to scale like $\mathcal{O}(n^3+k^3N_\text{mesh}^3)$.

The matrix inversion is simple Gauss-Jordan inversion, which is of complexity class (for an $n\times n$ matrix) $\mathcal{O}(n^3)$. \citet{Coppersmith:1987} showed that this could be optimised to $\mathcal{O}(n^{2.376})$ and conjectured reducibility to $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$.\footnote{The current ``best'' optimisation is $\mathcal{O}(n^{2.3727})$ \citep{Williams:2012}. DeepMind's AlphaTensor has claimed a faster result in \citet{Fawzi:2022}, though they do not state a complexity class. This is because ``...this does not yield practical algorithms.''} Since our code is embarassingly parallelisable, and can be executed in parallel simply by increasing the number of cores available, the payoff for decreasing the runtime by one polynomial order is inconsequential, and thus we use the straightforward implementation.

\subsubsection{Initial Condition Selection}

In this section we provide an account for the selection of our general initial conditions. Unless otherwise noted in this thesis, it is assumed that the values will be using the parameters described here and in \cref{chap:param_config}.

In our implementation of \gls{mlt} \citep{Vitense:1953, Bohm-vitense:1958, Cox:1968}, we use an \gls{mlt} parameter of $\alpha_\text{\gls{mlt}}=1.985$, selected via calibration against a model of the Sun. This is effectively a dissipativity parameter, and represents the depth at which a parcel of matter penetrates into a lower layer before becoming fully dissipated. The value was selected by modelling a \SI{1}{\msun} star and varying only the mixing length parameter until it had a lumonosity of \SI{1}{\lsun} and a radius of \SI{1}{\rsun}, at the present age of the Sun.

\subsubsection{Mesh spacing}

A natural question that arises when modelling a star is where internally to model it -- there is a counterbalance between the level of detail and the time cost of the matrix inversion. The obvious answer would be to distribute a mesh equally spaced within radius of the star, however the nonhomogeniety of a star, plus the potential for very thin shells of physically important material, necessitate a more intelligent approach.

Instead of being equally spaced \textit{radially}, these mesh points are taken to be equally spaced within a function $\mathcal{Q}$, which is taken from \citet{Stancliffe:2004} and given for a binary that does not have \gls{agb}-specific treatment enabled by

\begin{align}
    \mathcal{Q}\left(P, T, M, R\right) &= c_4\ln P + c_5\ln\left(P+c_\text{H}\right) + c_2\ln\left(P+3 P_\text{He}\right) + c_2\ln\left(P+0.3 P_\text{He}\right) + c_7\ln\left(\frac{T}{T+2\times 10^4}\right) \notag\\
    &\phantom{=} + \ln\left(\frac{c_6M^\frac23}{M + M^\frac23}\right) - c_3\ln\left(\frac{R^2}{c_8}+1\right),
\end{align}

where $P, T, M, R$ are the total pressures, temperatures, masses, and radii of each star, the constants $c_i$ are user controllable and given in \cref{chap:param_config}, $P_\text{He}$ is the pressure in the helium-burning shell, and

\begin{equation}
    c_\text{H} = \begin{cases}
        0.1 P_\text{H} & X_\text{H} \geq 0.15 \\
        0.1 \mathcal{P} & \text{otherwise}
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

where $P_\text{H}$ is the pressures in the hydrogen-burning shell. Here, $\mathcal{P}$ represents the pressure in the hydrogen-burning shell when $X_\text{H} = 0.15$. The choice of mesh spacing coefficients $c_1\dots c_{10}$ leads to different areas of the star being prioritised. Coordinates $c_1, c_9,$ and $c_{10}$ are not used except in \gls{agb}-specific evolution, which is not required for most cases (and requires a change of parameterisation in the pressure-shell terms). $c_5$ is hardcoded to be 0.15, and $c_2$ to be 0.0. \todo{but why? ``its what the code does'' is a bad reason.}

Placement of the mesh is controlled by the derivative $\pdv{\mathcal{Q}}{k}$, where $k$ is the meshpoint number \citep{Stancliffe:2016}. Specifically, meshpoints are placed at locations where

\begin{equation}
    \eval{\pdv{\mathcal{Q}}{k}}{k}-\eval{\pdv{\mathcal{Q}}{k}}{k+1} = 0.
\end{equation}

The mesh is thus adaptive (insofar as it changes to place meshpoints where they are needed for evolution) and non-Lagrangian (insofar as the derivative is not at constant mass coordinate). If one desires to make the mesh viscous (i.e., partially Lagrangian), one can follow \citet{Stancliffe:2004} and write, for $0\leq\gamma\leq1$,

\begin{equation}
    \pqty{\eval{\pdv{\mathcal{Q}}{k}}{k}-\eval{\pdv{\mathcal{Q}}{k}}{k+1}}\pqty{1-\gamma} + \gamma\pqty{\eval{\pdv{m}{t}}{k}} = 0,
\end{equation}

where now the parameter $\gamma$ is a weighting coefficient such that if $\gamma=1$, then the mesh spacing function is irrelevant and the parameters are spaced equally in mass, and if $\gamma=0$, the original mesh spacing function is used. In between, if $\gamma=\gamma\pqty{k, \dd{t}}$, then parts of the mesh are allowed to be Lagrangian and parts non-Lagrangian; the mesh is said to be \textit{viscous}. We typically keep the mesh non-viscous and non-Lagrangian, as a viscous mesh is most useful for \gls{agb} evolution and evolving through thermal pulses.

More detail is provided in \citet{Eggleton:1971} for the original mesh spacing function, and \citet{Stancliffe:2004} for the binary mesh spacing function.

\subsubsection{Timestep control}

The \codename{STARS} code utilises an adaptive timestep control mechanism, whereby the timestep is allowed to shrink to model rapid changes or grow if the star is broadly inert and unchanging. The core computation is a parameter $\Delta$, given by

\begin{equation}
    \Delta = \frac{d_\text{opt}}{\sum_i \sum_j \left|\delta x_{ij}\right|},\label{eq:timestep_selection}
\end{equation}

where $d_\text{opt}$ is a pre-defined target timestep (typically taken to be $\sim 1$, unless otherwise specified; see \cref{chap:param_config} for our selections), and $\delta x_{ij}$ is the change in variable $x_i$ at meshpoint $j$. In this calculation, luminosity is explicity excluded as this would cause the timestep to explode during certain points of the star's life-time. A new timestep, $d^\prime$, is selected from the previous timestep $d$ by

\begin{equation}
    d^\prime = \begin{cases}
        \min\left[d_1, \Delta\right]d & \Delta < 1 \\
        d & \Delta = 1 \\
        \max\left[d_2, \Delta\right]d & \Delta > 1
    \end{cases},
\end{equation}

where $d_1$ and $d_2$ are pre-determined control parameters. Most commonly we take $d_1=0.1$ and $d_2=1.2$ unless otherwise specified.

\subsubsection{Numerical instabilities}\label{sec:numerical_instabilities}

Stellar modelling is a nontrivial endeavour and there are many cases where the code can fail seemingly arbitrarily. The largest cause of numerical instability is the helium flash, which occurs in low-mass stars between about $\SI{0.8}{\msun} \lesssim M_\text{ZAMS} \lesssim \SI{2.5}{\msun}$. In this band, the triple-$\alpha$ process, given by the reactions \citep{Carroll:2017}\footnote{The reversible reaction is due to Beryllium-8 being unstable; its half-life is on the order of $\SI{1e-16}{\second}$, and it decays back to 2$^4_2$He if it does not react further.}

\begin{align*}
    \ce{^4_2He + ^4_2He &\rightleftharpoons ^8_4Be - \SI{0.0918}{\mega\electronvolt}}, \\
    \ce{^8_4Be + ^4_2He &-> ^12_6C + 2\gamma{} + \SI{7.367}{\mega\electronvolt}},\text{ and} \\
    \ce{^12_6C + ^4_2He &-> ^16_8O + \gamma{} + \SI{7.162}{\mega\electronvolt}},
\end{align*}

occurs within a highly degenerate helium core. As a result, the temperature of the star increases sharply during this process, leading to thermal runaway. This temperature increase in turn results in an increase in the luminosity (which peaks at $\sim\SI{1e10}{\lsun}$). The flash itself -- being the period of thermal runaway -- occurs on the order of a few minutes, though the spike in luminosity lasts approximately $\SI{7}{\mega\year}$ (for the $\SI{2.51}{\msun}$ star shown in \cref{fig:thermal_pulses_he_flash}).

A method termed ``pseudoevolution'' can be used to overcome this source of error. Briefly, a complete model (termed the \textit{reference model}) of a \SI{3}{\msun} star is evolved with a full model description saved at every timestep. When the helium flash occurs in a model (heuristically, $0.1 \leq M_\text{He} / \unit{\msun} \leq 0.55$ and $\log T_\text{c} / \unit{\kelvin} > 7.9$), we query the reference model for a model with the same helium core mass as the failed model. Using a custom subroutine in \codename{stars}, we turn off all nuclear and thermal burning and remove mass from the reference model to match the mass of the failed model. This model is then allowed to evolve as per normal, having overcome the helium flash. We note that this is not a \textit{perfect} method, as this does induce discontinuities in the plots, as shown in \cref{fig:pseudoevolved_kippenhahn}, though these are essentially insignificant.

The other stage where low-mass stars are prone to fail is during a thermal pulse \citep{Schwarzschild:1965, Schwarzschild:1967}. The mass range for thermal pulses is broader, from $\sim\SI{0.8}{\msun}$ to $\sim\SI{8}{\msun}$. A thermal pulse occurs as a star ascends the \gls{agb}; as a \gls{CO_core} or \gls{ONe_core} has developed, shells of burning helium and hydrogen (separated by a region of almost pure helium) sit atop the core \citep{Pols:2001}. As the intershell region dissipates due to dredge up or helium burning, hydrogen burning reignites in the hydrogen shell. As both shells burn (at the same rate), the helium-burning shell becomes unstable and eventually a process of thermal runaway begins (similar to the helium flash, except off-core). This causes a spike in the luminosity, termed a thermal pulse. This can persist for many pulses -- \citet{Stancliffe:2006} observes pulses before model termination, while \citet{Stancliffe:2005} observes 17 pulses for a model similar to those in \cref{fig:thermal_pulses_he_flash}.

The method to overcome this numerical instability is involved and not fully implemented in \codename{STARS}. Briefly, the number of meshpoints must be drastically increases near the onset of thermal pulses; up to 999 instead of the typical 199/499. The timestep must also be forcibly decreased; thermal pulses typically last for around a year \citep{Pols:2001} so the timestep must be reduced to not miss them (and to not have the modulus of the change in parameters -- $\sum_i \sum_j \left|\delta x_{ij}\right|$ in \cref{eq:timestep_selection} -- and thus the error, increase too much). This method was implemented to generate \cref{fig:thermal_pulses_he_flash}, though only two pulses were achievable.

\subsubsection{Critical modifications}

The major modifications we have made to the \codename{stars} code are as follows.

Firstly, the floating-point precision has been increased to double precision for all variables. This has minor effects on the evolved models, insofar as they are generally able to evolve to slightly further stages. This step has previously been undertaken, however not all variables were correctly converted. This did not invalidate any models previous versions of the \codename{stars} code has generated; rather it means they terminated slightly earlier than the point to which they may have been able to evolve.%they may have been able to evolve to.

As part of this, we also undertook a reasonably extensive refactor of the codebase. Whilst some of the code remains as originally written, it is now more compliant with modern programming practices and is in some sense easier to alter with new routines.

We have also improved mass loss interpolation in the \codename{massloss.f} routine. Whilst not changing the underlying assumption that mass transfer is governed by \citet{De_jager:1988, Vink:2001} (\cref{sec:mass_transfer}), we have improved the transition between typical O-type mass loss and WR mass loss rates by slowing turning on the WR mass loss rate as the surface hydrogen abundance drops below 0.4. The transition was previously abrupt and would cause the code to fail due to numerical instability if a non-WR star entered this regime.

Similarly to this, the code now manually reduces the timestep in the run-up to, during, and after the contact binary phase. Evolution during this phase breaks thermal equilibrium (i.e., the star evolves on a longer timescale than its Kelvin-Helmholtz time-scale) and hence the evolution can change rapidly as the star attempts to adjust back to thermal equilibrium. The contact phase, for example, typically last for between 100-200 \unit{\kilo\year} in our models.

We have also begun implementing an $\alpha-\lambda$ treatment of \gls{cee}, though we stress that this has not been rigorously tested yet, and is thus disabled for the majority of our models.

\subsection{The \codename{Tui} code}\label{chap:numerical_methods_tui}

\codename{Tui} is the core population synthesis code within the \gls{bpass} project. \codename{Tui} takes as input the output from the \codename{stars} code. \codename{Tui} was first described in \citet{Ghodla:2023}, and was further described in \citet{Stevance:2023}, though we note that their version is technically a refactor of the version we use. The predominant function we use \codename{Tui} for is the matching of pre- and post- supernova models for \cref{chap:natal_kicks}, and in this section we provide a brief account of that functionality of \codename{Tui} (though the control of the population synthesis models is beyond the scope of this thesis).

\codename{Tui} has precompiled ``input\_'' files which report a \codename{STARS} plot file, an \gls{imf} weight, and a model type (merger/normal primary/normal secondary/single model/quasi-homogeneous evolution binary/single star). After loading in a model, \codename{Tui} applies a given kick and computes the new orbital parameters from \citet{Tauris:1998}. Crucially, a core assumption of \gls{bpass} is that the orbit remains circular after the first supernova and is allowed to be eccentric after the second. Of course, nature cares little for our assumptions, and the supernova may cause the binary to become eccentric after the first supernova; \codename{Tui} accounts for this by setting the separation after the first supernova to be the semi-latus rectum of the eccentric binary. \codename{Tui} repeats this process after the second supernova.

For the purposes of \cref{chap:natal_kicks}, if the binary remains bound (has $0\leq e < 1$) after the second supernova, we record the following parameters:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The mass of the primary,
    \item The mass of the secondary,
    \item The orbital separation,
    \item The eccentricity,
    \item A weight term proportional to the \gls{imf} weighting,
    \item The rejuvenation age, which is the time since the companion last accreted material, if it accreted more than five per cent of its initial mass \citep[see][]{Richards:2023}, and
    \item The evolution age, which is the length of time from the secondary's \gls{zams} until its death.
\end{enumerate}

This provides us with the post-secondary supernova orbital parameters which determine the properties of the merger, such as the event rate $\mathcal{R}(z)$ and its path through period-eccentricity space.

\subsection{The \codename{Kaitiaki} grid-watcher code}\label{chap:numerical_methods_kaitiaki}

\codename{Kaitiaki} is a grid-watcher code we have developed that sits atop the \codename{STARS} code and manages its execution. It was developed primarily to manipulate the fixed-width text files that \codename{STARS} uses, in contrast to the ``typical'' manipulation done through the command line. However development proceeded in such a way that it now provides an interface to all of the (relevant to this thesis) output and input files. Further, it also now allows the user to control jobs through a \textsc{Python} interface, greatly simplifying grid calculations.

The core of \codename{Kaitiaki} is the \mintinline{Python}{STARSController()} interface.
