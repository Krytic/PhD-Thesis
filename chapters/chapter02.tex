\chapter{Theoretical Background}

\epigraph{Se'i lua'i lou le 'ulu taumamao. (Pick the breadfruits on the far-off branches first)}{S\=amoan proverb}

In this chapter, we provide a brief account of the evolution of a star from a theoretical perspective, from its formation to post-supernova. We will discuss both the case of a single isolated star, and an isolated binary. It is pertinent to note that our binaries will be assumed to have formed at birth, rather than by, for example, the dynamical capture of a runaway star in a globular cluster, which introduces extra effects and considerations. This process can be modelled by evolving a single star to a certain point, and then enabling binary evolution.

\section{Stellar Formation}\label{sec:stellar_formation}

\epigraph{Nature and nature's laws lay hid in night: God said, \textit{Let Newton be!} and all was light.}{Alexander Pope}

From the point of view of stellar evolution, which starts in earnest at the \gls{zams}, the formation of stars is interesting but ultimately broadly unimportant. We provide a short recap of stellar formation here as scene-setting, but our stellar evolution models assume that a gas cloud has already collapsed and we have a star at its \gls{zams}.

Stars are born in clouds of dust in rich H(II) regions of space. Due to inhomegeneities in the dust formation, some regions become more dense than others. When a region of this gas with adiabatic constant $\gamma$ and mean molecular weight $\mu$ reaches the so-called Jeans Mass,

\begin{equation}
    M_\text{J} = \pqty{\frac{10k_\text{B}T}{3\pqty{\gamma-1}\mu m_\text{H}G}}^\frac32 \pqty{\frac{3}{4\pi\rho}}^\frac12 \sim \pqty{\frac{10k_\text{B}T}{G}R}^\frac32 \pqty{\frac{1}{M}}^\frac12
\end{equation}

where the similarity is taking $\gamma=\frac53, \mu=\frac12$ for an adiabatic gas composed of H(II), then gravity becomes the dominant force in the clumps of gas. This causes a protostar to form. This protostar slowly gains more mass from its surroundings and, as it does so, increases in temperature. When the core temperature reaches $\SI[parse-numbers=false]{10^7}{\kelvin}$, the hydrogen in the core begins to fuse into helium via the reaction chain

\begin{align*}
    \ce{^2_1H + ^2_1H &-> ^3_2He + ^1_0n + \SI{3.27}{\mega\electronvolt}} \\
    \ce{^2_1H + ^3_2He &-> ^4_2He + ^1_1H + \SI{18.3}{\mega\electronvolt}}
\end{align*}

This is aided by two further hydrogen to helium reactions:

\begin{align*}
    \ce{^2_1H + ^2_1H &-> ^3_1He + ^1_1H + \SI{4.03}{\mega\electronvolt}} \\
    \ce{^2_1H + ^3_1H &-> ^4_2He + ^1_0n + \SI{17.59}{\mega\electronvolt}}
\end{align*}

or, via a master equation,

\begin{equation}
    \ce{6^2_1H -> 2^4_2He + 2^1_1H + 2^1_0n + \SI{43.2}{\mega\electronvolt}},
\end{equation}

with intermediate products which remain stable and are thus omitted for simplicity. This energy release will sustain the star throughout it's lifetime, providing outwards pressure resisting gravity pulling the star inwards, in a balance termed \textit{hydrostatic equilibrium}. Once hydrogen fusion has begun, we say that the star has reached \gls{zams}, and is now on the \textit{main sequence}, where it will spend approximately 90\% of its lifetime.

\section{Stellar Evolution}

\mediaepigraph{It did not last: the Devil howling "Ho! Let Einstein be!" restored the status quo.}{Answer to Pope's Epitaph for Sir Isaac Newton}{J. C. Squire}

Now that our protostar has evolved onto the main sequence, it has become a star in its own right. Over the next few million years, it will continue to burn hydrogen into helium, forming a Helium core. It is at this point that the evolution encounters a number of bifurcation points, depending on the mass and chemical composition of the star. In general, while the star lives, the four equations of stellar structure hold:

\begin{equation}
    \left.\begin{array}{ll}
        \dv{m}{r} &= 4\pi r^2\rho \\
        \dv{p}{r} &= -\frac{GM\rho}{r^2} \\
        \dv{T}{r} &= -\frac{3\kappa\rho L}{64\pi r^2\sigma T^3} \\
        \dv{L}{r} &= 4\pi r^2\rho\epsilon
    \end{array}\right\}
    \label{eq:stellar_structure}
\end{equation}

where $\epsilon$ is the rate of energy energation per unit mass, $\kappa$ is the opacity of the material, $\sigma$ is the Stefann-Boltzmann constant, and all other symbols have their usual meanings.

These equations represent conservation of mass, hydrostatic equilibrium, energy transport, and thermal equilibrium, respectively. Knowing constants $\epsilon, \kappa$, we have four equations for four unknowns. It is usually convenient to take the \textit{Rosseland mean} opacity,

\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{\kappa} = \frac{\pi}{acT^3}\int_0^\infty \frac{1}{\kappa_\nu}\pdv{B}{T}~\mathrm{d}\nu
\end{equation}

where $B$ is the black-body intensity at frequency $\nu$ and temperature $T$. The function $\kappa_\nu$ is obtainable from nuclear physics: it is intimately related to the scattering cross-section $\sigma$:

\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{\kappa_\nu \rho} = \frac{1}{\sigma n}
\end{equation}

where $\rho$ is the mass density of the star, and $n$ is the particle number density.

On the other hand, $\epsilon$ is obtainable from knowing the nuclear reactions occurring in the star's core:

\begin{equation}
    \epsilon = \sum_{i}^N \text{energy output of reaction } i
\end{equation}

All told, in the simplest sense, the internal \textit{structure} of the star can be modelled by hand by solving \autoref{eq:stellar_structure} from $m=0$ to $m=M$.

\subsection{The chemical composition of stars}

A star contains at its core a complex nuclear furnace. Since stars are born in regions of space rich in H(II) -- molecular hydrogen -- and obtain high enough pressures in the core to ignite fusion, the star will be sustained by the burning of predominantly Hydrogen and Helium. We thus group the three main stages into Hydrogen, Helium, and Metals (which we define as anything with atomic number $\geq3$, i.e., Lithium and heavier elements). It is convenient to speak of the mass fraction of the element, i.e., the fraction by mass of a star that is a certain element. For our purposes, this is expressible as

\begin{equation}
	X+Y+\sum_{i=0}^{115} \mathcal{Z}_i = 1, \label{eq:mass_fracs}
\end{equation}

where $X$ is the mass fraction of Hydrogen, $Y$ of Helium, and $\mathcal{Z}_i$ as the metal\footnote{It is custom in astronomy to refer to anything with an atomic number greater than 2 as a metal, rather than the conventional definition of a material conductive at absolute zero, including most of the d-block, s-block, f-block, and about half of the p-block on the periodic table.} with atomic number $i+3$. When speaking about the chemical composition of a star it is also convenient to speak of the \textit{metallicity}, the fraction at \gls{zams} of the star that is metal. For this, we define the metallicity as

\begin{equation}
    Z \equiv \sum_{i=0}^{115} \mathcal{Z}_i,
\end{equation}

such that \autoref{eq:mass_fracs} becomes

\begin{equation}
    X+Y+Z = 1.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Evolutionary Pathways}

In this section, we examine the four major regimes of stellar evolution for single-stars. The precise boundaries are debatable, but are generally accepted to be logarithmically spaced.

\subsubsection{Low-Mass Stars: \texorpdfstring{$\SI{0.08}{\msun} < M < \SI{2}{\msun}$}{Stars with between 0.08 and 2 times the mass of the sun}}

Low-mass stars ignite helium degenerately in their cores, leading to thermal runaway. The star can peak at a luminosity close to $10^{11} L_\odot$ (approximately 100 times the peak luminosity of SN 1987A, per Figure 5 of \citet{arnett_supernova_1989}). However, this peak in luminosity is attributable to nuclear fusion in the core, and the energy does not reach the surface, let alone escape into the \gls{ism} -- rather, it is absorbed into the outer layers of the star. During this extremely brief thermal runaway, the luminosity begins to settle and the star is able to continue evolving. Models of low mass stars fail at this stage and care is required to evolve them.

After the ignition of helium, these stars cross the so-called Hertzsprung Gap -- the region where very few stars are observed empirically. This region is bounded (on a colour-magnitude diagram, which is a transform of the HR diagram)\footnote{The relationship between a colour-magnitude diagram and the HR diagram is that $\log L=0.4\pqty{M_{\text{bol},\odot}-M_{\text{bol},*}}\simeq0.4\pqty{4.74-M_{v,*}-\text{BC}}$, $\log T=\log\pqty{4600\pqty{\frac{1}{0.92\pqty{B-V}+1.7}+\frac{1}{0.92\pqty{B-V}+0.62}}}$, where BC is a bolometric correction which depends on the stellar type. This expression for the temperature is a fit provided by \citet{ballesteros_new_2012}.} by the points $\pqty{M_v, B-V}=\left\{\pqty{2,0.5}, \pqty{-7, 0.1}, \pqty{-6, 1.6}\right\}$ \citep{hoyle_main-sequence_1960}, where $M_v$ is the V-band magnitude (magnitude in the visual band), and $B-V$ is the colour index of the star. The phenomenon of a gap existing is attributable to the fact that stars cross this gap exceedingly quickly: whereas it spends $\sim90\%$ of its life on the main sequence, it spends $\sim2\%$ of its life crossing the Hertzsprung gap \citep{hoyle_main-sequence_1960}.

\subsection{Relativistic corrections}

\epigraph{There's a trick you can use in mathematics called ``not worrying about it''}{Matt Parker}

This analysis has presumed Newtonian mechanics. However, the mass of stars puts them formally into the general relativistic regime, and thus strictly speaking we should treat this as a problem in full GR. In this approach, we would solve the Einstein Field Equations for the interior of the star:

\begin{equation}
    G_{\mu\nu} + \Lambda g_{\mu\nu} = \kappa T_{\mu\nu}
\end{equation}

with cosmological constant $\Lambda$, stress-energy $T_{\mu\nu}$, and the Einstein tensor $G_{\mu\nu}$ given by

\begin{align}
    G_{\mu\nu} &= R_{\mu\nu}-\frac12 g_{\mu\nu}R \\
    &= \tensor{R}{^\lambda_{\mu\lambda\nu}}-\frac12 g_{\mu\nu}\tensor{R}{^\sigma_\sigma} \\
    &= \tensor{R}{^\lambda_{\mu\lambda\nu}}-\frac12 g_{\mu\nu}g^{\sigma\gamma}R_{\sigma\gamma} \\
    &= \sum_\lambda\tensor{R}{^\lambda_{\mu\lambda\nu}}-\frac12 g_{\mu\nu}\sum_\sigma\sum_\gamma\sum_\alpha g^{\sigma\gamma}\tensor{R}{^\alpha_{\sigma\alpha\gamma}}\label{eq:reintroduce_summands} \\
    &= \sum_\lambda\pqty{\tensor{\Gamma}{^\lambda_{\mu\nu,\lambda}}-\tensor{\Gamma}{^\lambda_{\mu\lambda,\nu}}+\sum_\xi\tensor{\Gamma}{^\lambda_{\xi\lambda}}\tensor{\Gamma}{^ \xi_{\mu\nu}}-\sum_\xi\tensor{\Gamma}{^\lambda_{\xi\nu}}\tensor{\Gamma}{^\xi_{\mu\lambda}}} \notag\\
    &\phantom{=}-\frac12 g_{\mu\nu}\sum_\sigma\sum_\gamma\sum_\alpha g^{\sigma\gamma}\pqty{\tensor{\Gamma}{^\alpha_{\sigma\gamma,\alpha}}-\tensor{\Gamma}{^\alpha_{\sigma\alpha,\gamma}}+\sum_\xi\tensor{\Gamma}{^\alpha_{\xi\alpha}}\tensor{\Gamma}{^\xi_{\sigma\gamma}}-\sum_\xi\tensor{\Gamma}{^\alpha_{\xi\gamma}}\tensor{\Gamma}{^\xi_{\sigma\alpha}}},
\end{align}

where there is still tensorial shorthand:

\begin{equation}
    \tensor{\Gamma}{^\gamma_{\beta\mu}} \equiv \frac12\sum_\alpha g^{\alpha\gamma}\pqty{g_{\alpha\beta,\mu}+g_{\alpha\mu,\beta}-g_{\beta\mu,\alpha}}
\end{equation}

and the comma-derivative notation is

\begin{equation}
    \phi_{,\mu} \equiv \pdv{\phi}{x^\alpha},
\end{equation}

such that, for example,

\begin{equation*}
    g_{\alpha\beta,\mu} = \pdv{g_{\alpha\beta}}{x^\mu}.
\end{equation*}

In \autoref{eq:reintroduce_summands}, we explicitly reinsert the summation symbols to illustrate the number of simultaneous summations that are taking place implicitly. As one can see, this reasonably simple expression balloons quickly into something intractable by hand and time-consuming to evaluate computationally. Although the metric tensor is generally four-dimensional,\footnote{And thus the summation indices run from 1 to 4} such as the Friedmann-Lema\^itre-Robertson-Walker metric \citep{friedmann_uber_1922,lemaitre_homogeneous_1931,robertson_kinematics_1935,walker_milnes_1937} describing the expansion of the Universe,


\begin{equation*}
    g_{\mu\nu} = \begin{pmatrix}
            -1 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
            0 & a(t)^2 & 0 & 0 \\
            0 & 0 & r^2 & 0 \\
            0 & 0 & 0 & r^2\sin^2\theta
    \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation*}

it often lacks a closed form, especially on large-scale chaotic behaviour that cannot be locally ``washed out'' (such as how on Universal scales, individual stars can be ignored to describe the homogeneity and isotropy of the Universe), and is obtained via numerical methods.

This is also not considering complications of writing the stress-energy tensor $T_{\mu\nu}$, which may have its own complex form (again, assuming a closed-form solution \textit{exists}) determined via fluid-mechanical processes. All told, there are 64 equations to be solved instead of 4. In addition, the 64 solutions are ``worse'' to solve: harder to integrate by hand, and requiring greater time complexity. Numerical relativity codes typically simulate one star for the order of kiloseconds of its lifetime \citep[see, for an example,][]{ud-doula_first_2013}, and even the most efficient codes take longer than our codes to run. For instance, the \codename{Phantom} \gls{mhd} code \citep{price_phantom_2018} can take several days to run high-resolution models. Consequently, for the number of stars we wish to simulate, we choose to adopt a Newtonian framework.

A star's lifetime is, unfortunately, not infinite. The key assumption underpinning stellar evolution is that of hydrostatic equilibrium: the counterplay of gravity against outwards pressure. Whilst this equilibrium holds, the star is stable and resists explosion. It is when this equilibrium is broken -- with gravity the dominant force in this scenario -- that the star implodes in a \gls{ccsne}. From a thermodynamic perspective, as the star evolves, it shrinks as energy is bled off into the interstellar medium. This shrinkage compresses and heats the core, meaning the star's specific heat capacity is negative. This system is thermodynamically unstable, and thus every star either collapses into a remnant (c.f. black holes, neutron stars) or is sustained in some form by quantum mechanical phenomena (c.f. White Dwarfs, Thorne-\.Zytkow objects, and other exotica).

In any case, the breaking of hydrostatic equilibrium results generally results in\footnote{The extent of the veracity of this statement is an area of active research: \citet{reynolds_gone_2015} presents a short recap.} a supernova, which can be classified into subtypes, as shown in \autoref{fig:supernova_taxonomy}. In this event, gravity pulls the stellar core inwards, before ``bouncing'' and ejecting the envelope into the interstellar medium. The physics of this approximately second-long event are highly uncertain, and are in general unable to be modelled by detailed stellar evolution codes. All models we generate -- assuming they have not been terminated earlier -- will terminate at the moment of supernova.

Introducing a second star into the mix complicates the evolution. Whereas before a star losing mass (through, for example, a \gls{cme}) was assumed to eject it into the \gls{ism}, it may now accrete that material directly onto its companion. In addition, either star may in principle increase in radius enough to overlap with its companion, or even touch surfaces -- introducing new dynamics into the mix.

\subsection{Binary star kinematics}

There are several formation channels for a binary star system. Binaries may be formed from birth (as discussed in \autoref{sec:stellar_formation}, with the caveat that in this scenario two clumps of gas form near each other), via fragmentation of a protostar's disc, dynamical capture of one star by another (comparable to one of the suggested origins of Phobos and Deimos, per \citet{burns_dynamical_1978}), or via a higher multiplicity system ejecting one or more of its constituent stars, perhaps via a natal supernova kick.

In any case, once formed and the orbit is stabilised, binary systems obey Kepler's three laws. In the generalised binary regime, however, the assumption that $M_1\gg M_2$ (which underpins most statements of Kepler's laws) is not necessarily true -- for instance, the system LSS 3074 has two stellar components with \gls{zams} masses of approximately \SI{30}{\msun} and \SI{10}{\msun} \citep[][and \autoref{sec:close_contact} of this thesis]{raucq_observational_2017}. Moreover, the fact that there are two stars in orbit rather that one object orbiting another means that Kepler's laws must be stated more precisely. Formally, they can be expressed as (see the appendix of \citet{richards_missing_2021} for derivations)

\begin{enumerate}
    \item All orbits are ellipses, with both stars orbiting a common barycentre at the focus of the ellipse,
    \item A line segment joining the two stars sweeps out equal areas in equal times,
    \item The square of the period of the system is proportional to the cube of the semimajor axis of the ellipse (the binary separation), with the proportionality term dependent on the sum of the masses.
\end{enumerate}

The third law can be stated more precisely as,

\begin{equation}
    T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{G(M_1+M_2)}a^3,
\end{equation}

and a schematic of the orbit of a binary system is given in \autoref{fig:bss_schematic}.

Our master mass-loss rate is given by

\begin{align}
    \dot{M} &= \dot{M}_\text{Vink} - 10^3 M + \frac{M\left(1-\mathrm{d}M\right)}{\mathrm{d}t} + \min\left(\mathcal{R}\left(\frac{10R}{R_\text{RL}}\right)^3, 0.01\right) \notag\\
    &\phantom{=} - \min\left(\min\left[\mathcal{R}\left(\frac{10R}{R_\text{RL}}\right)^3, 0.01\right], \frac{M}{T_\text{KH}}\mathcal{R}\left(1-\frac{\omega}{0.8\omega_\text{crit}}\right)\right) \notag\\
    &\phantom{=}- \dot{M}_\text{Bondi}\label{eq:mass_loss_full}
\end{align}

where $\mathcal{R}(x)=\max\left(0, x\right)$ is the ramp function. This employs modified versions of the mass loss routines given in \citet{vink_mass-loss_2001}, as well as \citet{bondi_mechanism_1944,hurley_evolution_2002}. We detail the modifications we have made below. We take our accretion rates from \citet{bondi_mechanism_1944,hurley_evolution_2002}, limiting the accretion rate by the Kelvin-Helmholtz time-scale $T_\text{KH}$ of the star and by $1-\frac{\omega}{\omega_\text{crit}}$. The mean accretion rate (in the absence of RLOF) onto the companion is that given in \citet{bondi_mechanism_1944}, namely

\begin{equation}
    \dot{M}_\text{Bondi} = \min\left[-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-e^2}}\left(\frac{GM_2}{v_\text{W}^2}\right)^2 \frac{\alpha_\text{W}}{2a^2}\frac{1}{\left(1+v^2\right)^{3/2}}\dot{M}_\text{Vink}, 0.8\dot{M}_\text{Vink}\right],\label{eq:bondi}
\end{equation}

where we have assumed that

\begin{equation}
    v^2 = \frac{G\left(M_1+M_2\right)}{a v_\text{W}^2},
\end{equation}

and that

\begin{equation}
    v_\text{W} = 2\beta_\text{W}\frac{GM_1}{R_1}.
\end{equation}

The two free accretion parameters are assumed to be $\alpha_\text{W}=\frac32$ \citep{boffin_can_1988}, $\beta_\text{W}=7$ \citep{lamers_terminal_1995} for O-type stars. The computation for $\dot{M}_\text{Vink}$ is more involved, though follows the same essential structure as \citet{vink_mass-loss_2001}. We construct it as follows. The total pre-WR mass loss is given by \citet{de_jager_mass_1988}, namely

% \begin{equation}
%     \dot{M}_\text{pre-WR} = \dot{M}_\text{Vink} - \dot{M}_\text{RLF} + \langle \dot{M}_\text{2A}\rangle,\label{eq:pre-wr-ml}
% \end{equation}

\begin{align}
    \log\dot{M}_\text{de Jager} &= -\sum_{n=0}^5 \sum_{\substack{i=0\\j=n-i}}^n a_{ij}\cos\left(i\arccos x_1\right)\cos\left(j\arccos x_2\right),\\
    \dot{M}_\text{pre-WR} &= \sqrt{\frac{Z}{Z_\odot}}10^{\log\dot{M}_\text{de Jager}},\label{eq:pre-wr-ml}
\end{align}

where $x_1 = \frac{\log T-4.05}{0.75}$, $x_2=\frac{\log L-4.6}{2.1}$, and the coefficients $a_{ij}$ are given in Table VI of \citet{de_jager_mass_1988}. We interpolate the bi-stability jump around \SI{25}{\kilo\Kelvin} \citep[see, for example,][]{pauldrach_radiation-driven_1990, vink_nature_1999} with the following regime. Define the constants

\begin{align}
    R_1 &= \left(\frac{Z}{Z_\odot}\right)^{0.13},\label{eq:R1}\\
    R_2 &= -6.697+2.194\log\left(\frac{L}{10^5 L_\odot}\right)-1.313\log\left(\frac{M}{30M_\odot}\right)-1.226\log\left(1.3R_1\right)+0.933\log\left(\frac{T}{4\times10^4}\right) \notag\\
    &\phantom{=}-10.92\left(\log\left[\frac{T}{4\times 10^4}\right]\right)^2+0.85\log\left(\frac{Z}{Z_\odot}\right),\label{eq:R2} \\
    &\text{and} \notag\\
    R_3 &= -6.688+2.210\log\left(\frac{L}{10^5 L_\odot}\right)-1.339\log\left(\frac{M}{30M_\odot}\right)-1.601\log\left(0.65R_1\right)+1.07\log\left(\frac{T}{2\times 10^4}\right)\notag\\
    &\phantom{=}+0.85\log\left(\frac{Z}{Z_\odot}\right).\label{eq:R3}
\end{align}

 Our mass loss rate becomes:

\begin{equation}
    \dot{M}_\text{BS} = \begin{cases}
        \frac{(T-T_1)10^{R_3}+(T_2-T)\dot{M}_\text{pre-WR}}{2.5\times 10^3} & T_1 < T < T_2 \\
        10^{R_2} & T_2 \leq T \leq T_3 \\
        \frac{(T-T_3)10^{R_2}+(T_4-T)10^{R_3})}{5 \times 10^3} & T_3 < T < T_4 \\
        \frac{(6T_1-T)10^{R_2}+(T-5T_1)\dot{M}_\text{pre-WR}}{10^4} & 5T_1 < T < 6T_1 \\
        \dot{M}_\text{pre-WR} & \text{otherwise}
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

where $T_1=\SI{10}{\kilo\Kelvin}, T_2=\SI{12.5}{\kilo\Kelvin}, T_3=\SI{22.5}{\kilo\Kelvin}, T_4=\SI{27.5}{\kilo\Kelvin}$. This interpolation is shown in \autoref{fig:bistability}. We define

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{M} = \sqrt{\frac{Z}{Z_\odot}}\times 10^{-13.6 + 1.63\log\left(\frac{L}{L_\odot}\right)+2.22\log X},
\end{equation}

where $X$ is the surface hydrogen abundance of the primary, and then define

\begin{equation}
    \dot{M}_\text{WNL} = \begin{cases}
        5\left(\mathcal{M}-\dot{M}_\text{BS}\right)\left(0.42-X\right)+\dot{M}_\text{BS} & 0.42 > X > 0.4 \\
        \mathcal{M} & \text{otherwise}
    \end{cases}.
\end{equation}

Our total mass loss (provided the star has not depleted its surface helium, which will be discussed below) is now

\begin{equation}
    \dot{M}_\text{A} = \begin{cases}
        10\dot{\mu}\left(\log T-3.9\right) + \dot{M}_\text{BS} & X < 0.42 \text{ and } \log T > 3.9 \\
        \dot{M}_\text{WNL} & X > 0.42 \text{ and } \log T > 4.0 \\
        \dot{M}_\text{BS} & \text{otherwise}
    \end{cases},
\end{equation}

where $\dot{\mu}=\dot{M}_\text{WNL}-\dot{M}_\text{BS}$. Finally, if the surface helium depletes we modify the mass loss rate further, defining

\begin{equation}
    \dot{M}_\text{WC} = \sqrt{\frac{Z}{Z_\odot}}\times 10^{-8.3 + 0.84\log\left(\frac{L}{L_\odot}\right) + 2.04\log X_\text{He} + 1.04\log\left(1-X_\text{He}\right)}
\end{equation}

such that our total mass loss rate becomes (provided $X_\text{H} < 10^{-3}$ and $\log T > 4$)

\begin{equation}
    \dot{M}_\text{B} = \begin{cases}
        100\left(\dot{M}_\text{WC} - \dot{M}_\text{WNL}\right)\left(\mathcal{X}-0.02\right)+\dot{M}_\text{WNL} & \mathcal{X} > 0.02 \\
        \dot{M}_\text{WNL} & \mathcal{X} < 0.02
    \end{cases},
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{X}=\frac{\frac13 X_\text{C}+\frac14 X_\text{O}}{X_\text{He}}$. The total \citet{vink_mass-loss_2001} mass-loss rate is then

\begin{equation}
    \dot{M}_\text{Vink} = \begin{cases}
        \dot{M}_\text{B} & \text{surface helium depleted} \\
        \dot{M}_\text{A} & \text{otherwise}
    \end{cases}.\label{eq:vink_ml}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Roche Lobes}

\mediaepigraph[in]{Algebra’s like sheet music. The important thing isn’t can you read music, it’s can you \textit{hear} it. Can you hear the music, Robert?}{Niels Bohr}{Oppenheimer}

A binary system is complicated by the presence of its companions in several different ways. As alluded to in the previous section, the presence of a companion warps space-time in such a way that material can be quite distorted from a sphere or oblate spheroid but still remain gravitationally bound to the system. In the extremal case, a star can grow to fill or even exceed its Roche Lobe, which is the tear-drop equipotential shaped surface within which material remains gravitationally bound to its star. In \autoref{fig:roche_potentials} we display these equipotential surfaces for a range of different mass ratios. It is pertinent to note that the shape of all of the Roche Lobes change rapidly at low mass rations, however at higher mass ratios the shapes change less rapidly. In an equal-mass ratio binary, the binary configuration has rotational symmetry about the $\mathcal{L}_1$ point, and reflectional symmetry about the $x$-axis and the line $y=\mathcal{L}_1$.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{images/TheorySection/roche_potentials.png}
    \caption[The dimensionless Roche potential as a function of mass ratio $q$.]{The dimensionless Roche potential as a function of mass ratio $q$. Note that the shape of the Roche potential changes markedly at low mass ratio, and changes more slowly as the mass ratio approaches unity. Distances have been normalised such that the primary star is at $(x,y,z)=(0,0,0)$ and the secondary star is at $(x,y,z)=(1,0,0)$, i.e., we measure distances in units of separation and that the coordinate system is centered on the primary star.}
    \label{fig:roche_potentials}
\end{figure}

There are two primary Roche Lobe points we consider: the $\mathcal{L}_1$ point and the $\mathcal{L}_2$ point. The $\mathcal{L}_1$ point (also known as the inner Lagrangian point) is the nexus between the two stars, the $\mathcal{L}_2$ point (the outer Lagrangian point) is located on the other side of the secondary to the inner point.

We follow the derivation given in \citet{rose_advanced_1998} to find an expression for the Roche potential. First we note that in a rotating reference frame in Cartesian coordinates, we have

\begin{equation}
    \overbrace{\frac{GM_1 M_2}{a^2}\vu{x}}^\text{gravitational force}=\underbrace{-M_1 \vec{\omega}\times\pqty{\vec{\omega}\times\vec{r}_1}}_\text{centrifugal force},
\end{equation}

where $\vu{x}$ is the line between the center of mass of both stars, $a$ the separation, $\vec{\omega}$ the angular velocity, and $\vec{r}_1$ the position vector of the primary star. The x-ordinates of the centres of mass are given by

\begin{equation}
    \left.\begin{array}{ll}
        x_1 &= \frac{M_2}{M_1+M_2}a \\
        x_2 &= \frac{-M_1}{M_1+M_2}a
    \end{array}\right\}
\end{equation}

Since we are considering a rotating coordinate system we have that the centrifugal force is given by

\begin{equation}
    -M_1 \vec{\omega}\times\pqty{\vec{\omega}\times\vec{r}_1} = M_1\omega^2 x_1
\end{equation}

and hence

\begin{align}
    \frac{GM_1 M_2}{a^2}\vu{x} &= M_1\omega^2 x_1 \notag\\
    \omega^2 &= \frac{GM_1 M_2}{M_1 x_1 a^2}\vu{x} \notag\\
    &= \frac{GM_2}{x_1 a^2}\vu{x} \notag\\
    &= \frac{GM_2\pqty{M_1+M_2}}{M_2 \cdot a\cdot a^2}\vu{x} \notag\\
    &= \frac{G\pqty{M_1+M_2}}{a^3}\vu{x}.
\end{align}

The centrifugal potential of a test mass $m$ at $(x,y)$ is hence

\begin{equation}
    V = -\frac12 m\omega^2\pqty{x^2+y^2} = -\frac{Gm\pqty{M_1+M_2}\pqty{x^2+y^2}}{2a^3},
\end{equation}

and thus its energy is

\begin{equation}
    E = \frac12 m\pqty{\dot{x}^2+\dot{y}^2} + V(x,y),
\end{equation}

where

\begin{equation}
    V(x,y) = \overbrace{-\frac{GmM_1}{\sqrt{\pqty{x-x_1}^2+y^2}}}^\text{G.P.E. due to primary}-\overbrace{\frac{GmM_2}{\sqrt{\pqty{x-x_2}^2+y^2}}}^\text{G.P.E. due to secondary}-\overbrace{\frac{Gm\pqty{M_1+M_2}\pqty{x^2+y^2}}{2a^3}}^\text{centrifugal potential}.
\end{equation}

We introduce the dimensionless coordinates measured in units of the separation of the binary (or reduced masses)

\begin{equation}
    \xi = \frac{x}{a},\quad\eta=\frac{y}{a},\quad\xi_1=\frac{M_2}{M_1+M_2},\quad\xi_2=\frac{M_1}{M_1+M_2}
\end{equation}

Our potential now becomes

\begin{equation}
    V(\xi,\eta) = -\frac{Gm\pqty{M_1+M_2}}{a}\times\bqty{\frac{\xi_2}{\sqrt{\pqty{\xi-\xi_1}^2+\eta^2}}+\frac{\xi_1}{\sqrt{\pqty{\xi-\xi_2}^2+\eta^2}}+\frac12\pqty{\xi^2+\eta^2}}.
\end{equation}

Taking the partial derivative along the $\xi$-axis (the rescaled $x$-axis), we get

\begin{equation}
    \pdv{V}{\xi} = -\frac{Gm\pqty{M_1+M_2}}{a}\times\bqty{-\frac{\xi_2\pqty{\xi-\xi_1}}{\pqty{\pqty{\xi-\xi_1}^2+\eta^2}^\frac32}-\frac{\xi_1\pqty{\xi-\xi_2}}{\pqty{\pqty{\xi-\xi_2}^2+\eta^2}^\frac32}+\xi}
\end{equation}

Hence the extrema of the potential $V$ are at the points $\xi$ where (taking $\eta=0$ on the $\xi$-axis)

% -ve sign on first two terms dumbass

\begin{equation}
    0=\xi-\frac{\xi_2}{\pqty{\xi-\xi_1}^2}-\frac{\xi_1}{\pqty{\xi-\xi_2}^2}.\label{eq:roche_lobe_condition}
\end{equation}

% i.e.,

\section{Post-Supernova}\label{sec:post_SN}

\mediaepigraph{Wit nodded.\\
``Oh, is this the place where I talk?'' Kaladin said.\\
``If you wish.''\\
``I don’t wish. Get on with the story.''}{Rhythm of War}{Brandon Sanderson}

After a supernova, the star leaves behind its core as a compact remnant. Generally, these are either white dwarves, black holes, or neutron stars. The interior structure of each is either complicated or, in the case of black holes, impossible to observationally probe without violating causality. Their orbital motions are now deterministic from four initial parameters: $m_1, m_2, a$, and $e$. Motion is now wholly determined by the emission of gravitational radiation through (detectable) gravitational waves. The pertinent equations of motion are given by \citet{peters_gravitational_1964}, namely,

\begin{align}
    \dv{a}{t} &= \frac{-\beta}{a^3\left(1-e^2\right)^\frac72}\left(1+\frac{73}{24}e^2+\frac{37}{96}e^4\right) \label{eq:dadt}\\
    \dv{e}{t} &= -\frac{19}{12}\frac{\beta}{a^4\left(1-e^2\right)^\frac52}\left(e+\frac{121}{304}e^3\right)\label{eq:dedt}
\end{align}

with the constant $\beta$ defined as

\begin{equation}
    \beta \equiv \frac{64}{5}\frac{G^3mM\pqty{m+M}}{c^5}.
\end{equation}

Now if one defines the constant $c_0$ through

\begin{equation}
    c_0 = a_0\pqty{1-e_0^2}e_0^{-\frac{12}{19}}\pqty{1+\frac{121}{304}e_0^2}^{-\frac{870}{2299}},
\end{equation}

then the time from the second supernova to the conclusion of the inspiral, termed the \textit{coalescence time}, will be given by

\begin{equation}
    T_\text{c}(a_0, e_0) = \frac{12}{19}\frac{c_0^4}{\beta}\int_0^{e_0} \frac{e^\frac{22}{19}\left(1+\frac{121}{304}e^2\right)^\frac{1181}{2299}}{\left(1-e^2\right)^\frac32}{ \mathrm{d}e}.\label{eq:coalescence_time}
\end{equation}

The integral has no clean closed-form solution, and a numerical integrator is typically used to compute the coalescence time. However, some approximations such as \citet{mandel_accurate_2021} do exist and approximate the coalescence time rather well over the entire range of mass ratios.

\subsection{Merger Rates of compact binaries}

The merger rate of a population of compact binaries at time $t$ after formation can be formalised via the heuristic

\begin{equation}
    m(t) = \text{\# of binaries for which $T_\text{c} + T_\text{rej} + T_\text{evo} < t$},\label{eq:definition_of_merger_rate}
\end{equation}

where $T_\text{C}$ is the coalescence time, $T_\text{rej}$ is the rejuvenation age (the age at which the primary star last accreted mass), and $T_\text{evo}$ is the evolution time-scale (the length of time the primary spends on the main sequence). $m(t)$ is called the \gls{dtd} of a population.

However, the underlying assumption in this is that the population was formed after an instantaneous burst of star-forming activity at time $t=0$, which is clearly unphysical. We must account for the so-called \gls{sfrd} $\psi(z)$, assumed to be taken from \citet{madau_cosmic_2014}:

\begin{equation}
    \psi(z) = 0.015\frac{(1+z)^{2.7}}{1+\left(\frac{1+z}{2.9}\right)^{5.6}}~\unit{\msun\per\year\per\mega\pc\cubed},
\end{equation}

where $z$ is the redshift. For the purposes of converting between lookback time $t$ and redshift $z$, we assume a standard LCDM cosmology with $\Omega_\text{M}=0.3, \Omega_\Lambda=0.7, \Omega_k = 0, H_0=\SI{69.6}{\km\per\second\per\mega\pc}$.

Of course, this ignores the effect of mixed-metallicity models, which we account for using the presciption of \citet{langer_collapsar_2006}, whereupon the \gls{sfrd} is multiplied by a factor $\chi$:

\begin{equation}
    \chi\left(z,Z\right) = \frac{\hat{\Gamma}\left[0.84, \left(\frac{Z}{Z_\odot}\right)^2\cdot 10^{0.3z}\right]}{\Gamma\left(0.84\right)},
\end{equation}

where $\hat{\Gamma}$ and $\Gamma$ are the upper incomplete and complete Gamma functions respectively. This means our \gls{sfh} -- which takes into account both redshift and metallicity -- denoted $\Psi(z,Z)$, is given by

\begin{equation}
    \Psi(z,Z) = 0.015\int_{z_1}^{z_2} \frac{(1+z)^{2.7}}{1+\left(\frac{1+z}{2.9}\right)^{5.6}}\frac{\hat{\Gamma}\left[0.84, \left(\frac{Z}{Z_\odot}\right)^2\cdot 10^{0.3z}\right]}{\Gamma\left(0.84\right)}~\mathrm{d}z,
\end{equation}

where $z_1$ and $z_2$ are the redshifts of our bin edges. The event rate as a function of time, $\mathcal{R}(t)$, is therefore given by

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{R}(t) = \Psi(z_t) * m(t),\label{eq:event_rate_distribution} %= \int_0^\infty m(z_t) \cdot 0.015\int_{z_1}^{z_2} \frac{(1+z-z')^{2.7}}{1+\left(\frac{1+z-z'}{2.9}\right)^{5.6}}\frac{\hat{\Gamma}\left[0.84, \left(\frac{Z}{Z_\odot}\right)^2\cdot 10^{0.3(z-z')}\right]}{\Gamma\left(0.84\right)}~\mathrm{d}z~\mathrm{d}z',
\end{equation}

where $z_t$ is the redshift corresponding to lookback time $t$. Detail on how this convolution is computed may be found in \citet{richards_missing_2021}.

\section{A Final Note: Stellar Atmospheres}

A star is not a planet -- there is no firm boundary demarcating the inner of a star from the outer. The common definition of the ``surface'' of a star is the surface of last scattering (photosphere), which is the point radially in which the mean free path of a photon emitted from the core of the star becomes meaningfully long (internally, $\ell_\gamma\sim\SI{2}{\cm}$, externally, $\ell_\gamma\sim\SI{4.4}{\pc}$).\footnote{The mean free path is a function of the particle under consideration: for a neutrino, internally $\ell_\nu\sim\SI{3.68}{\pc}$ and externally $\ell_\nu\sim\SI{81}{\ronna\pc}\sim 3\times10^{18}$ times the diameter of the observable Universe.} However, outflows from the star as well as the potential interaction of a partner in a common-envelope event mean that this surface is not necessarily firm: there is no sharp turn from stellar material to empty space. Thus, the simulation of stellar atmospheres is of interest to the stellar astrophysicist. However, this introduces another rather large layer of complexity. The synthesis of a stellar spectra, for example, is not a trivial task, as well as simulating the matter content. This thesis is concerned with detailed modelling of the stellar \textit{interior}, and thus the atmosphere is an interesting sidenote, but one we shall not pursue.

\subsubsection{Brief History}

Stellar modelling became a field of active research in the 1950s. Prior to the invention of the computer, a model was treated as a mathematical process and thus was computed -- often labouriously -- by hand. For instance, in \citet{eggleton_structure_1967}, Eggleton writes that

\begin{fanciful}
    Since we have seen in Section 3 that the structure of the flame zone can be obtained independently (almost) of the rest of the star, it is reasonable to expect that instabilities of the type considered by Schwarzchild \& H\"arm can also be examined in this way, so that the considerable labour of computing the structure of an entire star can be avoided.
    \begin{flushright}
        -- \citet{eggleton_structure_1967}
    \end{flushright}
\end{fanciful}

The Cambridge \codename{stars} code was one of the first detailed stellar evolution codes to be written, though initially it only modelled one star in detail. To model a binary system, tweaks were made whereupon the primary would be modelled in detail whilst approximating the secondary with the equations of \citet{hurley_evolution_2002} until the primary supernova, at which point the secondary would be evolved in detail. This had its merits; notably computation time was saved, and the results were generally accurate anyway.

However, \citet{stancliffe_modelling_2009} introduced detailed modelling of \textit{both} stars in a binary system into the \codename{stars} code -- that is, the interiors of both stars were computed in detail, alongside the simualtaneous computation of the orbital parameters. Since then, a wide array of improvements have been made to the numerical stability of the code, as well as introducing or refining new physics.

There are four variants of this code:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The Cambridge \codename{stars} code, which is the version originally developed by Peter Eggleton and originally described in \citet{eggleton_evolution_1971}. This version was originally only capable of solving the equations for a single star.
    \item The \codename{Eldridge stars} code described in \citet{eldridge_progenitors_2004,Eldridge:2004a, eldridge_binary_2017}, which is the version used to generate the fiducial \gls{bpass} v2 grids. This version models the primary in detail while approximating the secondary using the equations of \citet{hurley_evolution_2002}.
    \item The \codename{bstars} code, which is the unmodified version lately available from Cambridge, described in \citet{stancliffe_modelling_2009}. This version models both stars in detail throughout their lives.
    \item The \codename{Aotearoa stars} code, which is the version of the code incorporating the changes we have made.
\end{enumerate}

Unless context otherwise requires, when we refer to ``the \codename{stars} code'' with no further qualification, we are referring specifically to the \codename{Aotearoa stars} code.

\subsubsection{Matrix Inversion}

The heart of \codename{stars} is a matrix inversion algorithm. Although computing the inverse of a matrix is a polynomial time process, it can still take considerable time to invert. \codename{stars} implements a Henyey matrix \citep{henyey_new_1964}, which is of size $n=(4+n_\text{comp})N_\text{mesh}$, with $n_\text{comp}$ the number of equations to be solved and $N_\text{mesh}$ the number of meshpoints in the model. An individual element of the Henyey matrix is given by

\begin{equation}
    \mat{H} = \pdv{\pqty{\text{equation } i}}{\pqty{\text{variable } j}}.
\end{equation}

For a ``typical'' binary computation, with $n_\text{comp}=30$ equations and $N_\text{mesh}=199$ meshpoints, the matrix is already $6766\times6766$, for a binary which evolves up the \gls{agb} (the theoretically most complex calculation we perform) with $N_\text{mesh}=999$, the matrix is $33966\times33966$. Introducing $k$ new equations into the code causes the runtime of the matrix inversion calculation to scale like $\mathcal{O}(n^3+k^3N_\text{mesh}^3)$.

The matrix inversion is simple Gauss-Jordan inversion, which is of complexity class (for an $n\times n$ matrix) $\mathcal{O}(n^3)$. \citet{coppersmith_matrix_1987} showed that this could be optimised to $\mathcal{O}(n^{2.376})$ and conjectured reducibility to $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$.\footnote{The current ``best'' optimisation is $\mathcal{O}(n^{2.3727})$ \citep{williams_multiplying_2012}. DeepMind's AlphaTensor has claimed a faster result in \citet{fawzi_discovering_2022}, though they do not state a complexity class. This is because ``...this does not yield
practical algorithms.''} Since our code is embarassingly parallelisable, and can be executed in parallel simply by increasing the number of cores available, the payoff for decreasing the runtime by one polynomial order is inconsequential, and thus we use the straightforward implementation.

\subsubsection{Initial Condition Selection}

In this section we provide an account for the selection of our general initial conditions. Unless otherwise noted in this thesis, it is assumed that the values will be using the parameters described here and in \autoref{chap:param_config}.

In our implementation of \gls{mlt} \citep{vitense_wasserstoffkonvektionszone_1953,bohm-vitense_uber_1958,cox_principles_1968}, we use an \gls{mlt} parameter of $\alpha_\text{\gls{mlt}}=1.985$, selected via calibration against a model of the Sun. This is effectively a dissipativity parameter, and represents the depth at which a parcel of matter penetrates into a lower layer before becoming fully dissipated. The value was selected by modelling a \SI{1}{\msun} star and varying only the mixing length parameter until it had a lumonosity of \SI{1}{\lsun} and a radius of \SI{1}{\rsun}, at the present age of the Sun.

\subsubsection{Timestep control}

The \codename{STARS} code utilises an adaptive timestep control mechanism, whereby the timestep is allowed to shrink to model rapid changes or grow if the star is broadly inert and unchanging. The core computation is a parameter $\Delta$, given by

\begin{equation}
    \Delta = \frac{d_\text{opt}}{\sum_i \sum_j \left|\delta x_{ij}\right|}\label{eq:timestep_selection}
\end{equation}

where $d_\text{opt}$ is a pre-defined target timestep (typically taken to be $\sim 1$, unless otherwise specified; see \autoref{chap:param_config} for our selections), and $\delta x_{ij}$ is the change in variable $x_i$ at meshpoint $j$. In this calculation, luminosity is explicity excluded as this would cause the timestep to explode during certain points of the star's life-time. A new timestep, $d^\prime$, is selected from the previous timestep $d$ by

\begin{equation}
    d^\prime = \begin{cases}
        \min\left[d_1, \Delta\right]d & \Delta < 1 \\
        d & \Delta = 1 \\
        \max\left[d_2, \Delta\right]d & \Delta > 1
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

$d_1$ and $d_2$ are pre-determined control parameters, most commonly we take $d_1=0.1$ and $d_2=1.2$ unless otherwise specified.

\subsubsection{Numerical instabilities}

Stellar modelling is a nontrivial endeavour and there are many cases where the code can fail seemingly arbitrarily. The largest cause of numerical instability is the helium flash, which occurs in low-mass stars between about $\SI{0.8}{\msun} \lesssim M_\text{ZAMS} \lesssim \SI{2.5}{\msun}$. In this band, the triple-$\alpha$ process, given by the reactions \citep{carroll_introduction_2017}\footnote{The reversible reaction is due to Beryllium-8 being unstable; its half-life is on the order of $\SI{1e-16}{\second}$, and it decays back to 2$^4_2$He if it does not react further.}

\begin{align*}
    \ce{^4_2He + ^4_2He &\rightleftharpoons ^8_4Be - \SI{0.0918}{\mega\electronvolt}} \\
    \ce{^8_4Be + ^4_2He &-> ^12_6C + 2\gamma{} + \SI{7.367}{\mega\electronvolt}} \\
    \ce{^12_6C + ^4_2He &-> ^16_8O + \gamma{} + \SI{7.162}{\mega\electronvolt}}
\end{align*}

occurs within a highly degenerate helium core. As a result, the temperature of the star increases sharply during this process, leading to thermal runaway. This temperature increase in turn results in an increase in the luminosity (which peaks at $\sim\SI{1e10}{\lsun}$). The flash itself -- being the period of thermal runaway -- occurs on the order of a few minutes, though the spike in luminosity lasts approximately $\SI{7}{\mega\year}$ (for the $\SI{2.51}{\msun}$ star shown in \autoref{fig:thermal_pulses_he_flash}).

A method termed ``pseudoevolution'' can be used to overcome this source of error. Briefly, a complete model (termed the \textit{reference model}) of a \SI{3}{\msun} star is evolved with a full model description saved at every timestep. When the helium flash occurs in a model (heuristically, $0.1 \leq M_\text{He} / \unit{\msun} \leq 0.55$ and $\log T_\text{c} / \unit{\kelvin} > 7.9$), we query the reference model for a model with the same helium core mass as the failed model. Using a custom subroutine in \codename{stars}, we turn off all nuclear and thermal burning and remove mass from the reference model to match the mass of the failed model. This model is then allowed to evolve as per normal, having overcome the helium flash. We note that this is not a \textit{perfect} method, as this does induce discontinuities in the plots, as shown in \autoref{fig:pseudoevolved_kippenhahn}, though these are essentially insignificant.

The other stage where low-mass stars are prone to fail is during a thermal pulse \citep{schwarzschild_thermal_1965,schwarzschild_hydrogen_1967}. The mass range for thermal pulses is broader, from $\sim\SI{0.8}{\msun}$ to $\sim\SI{8}{\msun}$. A thermal pulse occurs as a star ascends the \gls{agb}; as a \gls{CO_core} or \gls{ONe_core} has developed, shells of burning helium and hydrogen (separated by a region of almost pure helium) sit atop the core \citep{pols_thermal_2001}. As the intershell region dissipates due to dredge up or helium burning, hydrogen burning reignites in the hydrogen shell. As both shells burn (at the same rate), the helium-burning shell becomes unstable and eventually a process of thermal runaway begins (similar to the helium flash, except off-core). This causes a spike in the luminosity, termed a thermal pulse. This can persist for many pulses -- \citet{stancliffe_numerical_2006} observes pulses before model termination, while \citet{stancliffe_evolution_2005} observes 17 pulses for a model similar to those in \autoref{fig:thermal_pulses_he_flash}.

The method to overcome this numerical instability is involved and not fully implemented in \codename{STARS}. Briefly, the number of meshpoints must be drastically increases near the onset of thermal pulses; up to 999 instead of the typical 199/499. The timestep must also be forcibly decreased; thermal pulses typically last for around a year \citep{pols_thermal_2001} so the timestep must be reduced to not miss them (and to not have the modulus of the change in parameters -- $\sum_i \sum_j \left|\delta x_{ij}\right|$ in \autoref{eq:timestep_selection} -- and thus the error, increase too much). This method was implemented to generate \autoref{fig:thermal_pulses_he_flash}, though only two pulses were achievable.

\subsection{The \codename{Tui} code}\label{chap:numerical_methods_tui}

\codename{Tui} is the core population synthesis code within the BPASS project. \codename{Tui} takes as input the output from the \codename{stars} code. \codename{Tui} was first described in \citet{ghodla_evaluating_2023}, and was more fulsomely described in \citet{stevance_end--end_2023}, though we note that their version is technically a refactor of the version we use. The predominant function we use \codename{Tui} for is the matching of pre- and post- supernova models for \autoref{chap:natal_kicks}, and in this section we provide a brief account of that functionality of \codename{Tui} (though the control of the population synthesis models is beyond the scope of this thesis).

\codename{Tui} has precompiled ``input\_'' files which report a \codename{STARS} plot file, an \gls{imf} weight, and a model type (merger/normal primary/normal secondary/single model/quasi-homogeneous evolution binary/single star). After loading in a model, \codename{Tui} applies a given kick and computes the new orbital parameters from \citet{tauris_runaway_1998}. Crucially, a core assumption of \gls{bpass} is that the orbit remains circular after the first supernova and is allowed to be eccentric after the second. Of course, the supernova may cause the binary to become eccentric after the first supernova; \codename{Tui} accounts for this by setting the separation after the first supernova to be the semi-latus rectum of the eccentric binary. \codename{Tui} repeats this process after the second supernova.

For the purposes of \autoref{chap:natal_kicks}, if the binary remains bound after the second supernova, we record the following parameters:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The mass of the primary,
    \item The mass of the secondary,
    \item The orbital separation,
    \item The eccentricity,
    \item A weight term proportional to the \gls{imf} weighting,
    \item The rejuvenation age, which is the time since the companion last accreted material, if it accreted more than five per cent of its initial mass \citep[see][]{richards_new_2023}, and
    \item The evolution age, which is the length of time from the secondary's \gls{zams} until its death.
\end{enumerate}

This provides us with the post-secondary supernova orbital parameters which determine the properties of the merger, such as the event rate $\mathcal{R}(z)$ and its path through period-eccentricity space.

\subsection{The \codename{Kaitiaki} grid-watcher code}\label{chap:numerical_methods_kaitiaki}

\codename{Kaitiaki} is a grid-watcher code that sits atop the \codename{STARS} code and manages its execution. It was developed primarily to manipulate the fixed-width text files that \codename{STARS} uses, in contrast to the ``typical'' manipulation done through the command line.

The core of \codename{Kaitiaki} is \mintinline{Python}{STARSController()} interface. The most na\"ive run would be to initialise a model of, say, \SI{8}{\msun} and evolve it until it evolves up the \gls{agb}.
