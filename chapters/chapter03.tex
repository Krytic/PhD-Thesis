\chapterEpigraph{All hands, this is the captain. Begin prepping all stations for first contact.}{Captain Ed Mercer}

\lettrine[lines=3]{S}{ince the force between two} objects due to gravity scales like $F\sim\frac{1}{r_\text{sep}}$, another question naturally arises during the consideration of contact binaries. For sufficiently close binaries, one can imagine that mass transfer through \gls{rlof} or tidal effects could become reasonably strong, stripping the hydrogen envelope of a main sequence (or shortly post-main sequence) star and exposing the helium-enriched core underneath. If this stripping does not cause the star to retreat within its Roche lobe, then this core material would continue to be accreted, creating helium-enriched matter streams accreting onto its companion.

As discussed in \Cref{sec:binary_evolution}, the presence of a companion measurably alters the evolution of a star. Typically this manifests as allowing matter to be exchanged between the components or the gravity of one star warping the photosphere of the other. In this chapter we focus on the first instance, where mass transfer occurs between two stars. In particular, we consider a binary system with \gls{zams} masses of \SI[parse-numbers=false]{30+10}{\msun}, and an initial period of $10^{0.6}\approx$ \SI{3.98}{\day}. We specifically explore this case as a case study on the effect of helium enriched mass transfer. Future work will be needed to examine the prevalence of the effect of this mass transfer on a grid of close binaries.

The general background for this section remains as elaborated in \Cref{chap:theoretical_background}, however our parameterisation requires us to delinate between two cases: that when variable composition accretion is enabled and the case where it is disabled. These correspond to setting the parameter IVARACC in \Cref{chap:param_config} to 1 or 0 respectively.

\section{Evolution overview}

The major stages of the binary's evolution can be broken up into five main stages, explored in the following section. Each stage is assigned a label, which is delineated in \Cref{tab:binary_stages} -- in brief, the lexicographic ordering reflects temporal evolution of the binary. That is to say, (A) represents the \gls{zams}, (B) the start of \gls{rlof} on the donor, (C) the start of \gls{rlof} for the accretor, (D) the end of \gls{rlof} for the accretor, and (E) the end of \gls{rlof} for the donor. Unless otherwise indicated, at every stage we shall be discussing the variable composition accretion case. The full evolution for the variable (non-variable) accretion case is shown in \Cref{fig:main_evolution} (\Cref{fig:main_evolution_STANDARD}), with subplots showing the behaviour around the contact phase.

\subsection[Pre-interaction]{Pre-interaction (stages A--B)}\label{sec:pre_interaction_stages_a_b_}

The pre-interaction phase of both stars is identical in both cases, as the key change enabling this work only begins when \gls{rlof} occurs. In both cases, it evolves as a fully detached binary for \SI{4.51}{\mega\year}, with the donor almost completing its main sequence evolution. Hydrogen is exhausted in the core, and a helium core develops. In both cases, as the donor nears the Hertzsprung gap hook, it inflates to fill its Roche lobe, causing matter to begin to be exchanged via \gls{rlof} and kick-starting the pre-contact phase.

\subsection[Pre-contact]{Pre-contact (stages B--C)}\label{sec:pre_contact_stages_b_c_}

When the donor swells to fill its Roche lobe, it begins to efficiently transfer material to its companion. The increased drag causes the orbit to become hardened; shrinking by approximately 23 per cent. The accretor begins to accrete material, bleeding angular momentum into the system and causing the accretor to spin up. As the contact phase nears, the accretor approaches 40 per cent of the critical rotation rate, as shown in \Cref{fig:stellar_profiles}. It reaches approximately 20 per cent when the contact phase begins.

\subsection[Contact phase]{Contact phase (stages C--D)}\label{sec:contact_phase_stages_c_d_}

When the primary star exceeds its Roche lobe during its main sequence, it begins to efficiently transfer material onto the secondary. This mass transfer shrinks the orbital separation of the binary by about 23 per cent, and causes the secondary to swell and fill its own Roche lobe, forming a contact binary. The evolution in the lead up to, during, and immediately after the contact phase is shown in \Cref{fig:main_evolution}. Notably, when the system becomes a contact binary, the separation shrinks dramatically owing to conservation of angular momentum. This results in a final period of $\sim\SI{2.8}{\day}$.

Because of the short initial period of the binary, this stellar model is an example of a system that experiences a contact phase. This is due to the large amount of mass transferred in the immediate lead-up to this phase, causing the accretor to react by increasing in radius. The process of generating and sustaining the contact phase can be broken down into the following stages, summarised in \Cref{fig:stellar_profiles}:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item In the immediate lead-up to the contact phase, the accretor begins to accrete material, causing it to spin up. The rotation rate also increases due to transfer of angular momentum and the accretor approaches 40 per cent of its critical rotation rate.
    \item This accretion breaks thermal equilibrium in the accretor and causes it to expand \citep[][and references therein]{Lau:2024}
    \item The donor dims slightly as the accretor brightens by 63.4 per cent.
    \item The donor then begins to increase in luminosity, but the radius returns to approximately the size it was prior to the onset of RLOF, which is below the expected radius for a star of that luminosity.
    \item The disruption of thermal equilibrium in the accretor powers the contact phase, which lasts for approximately \SI{5}{\kilo\year}.
\end{enumerate}

Eventually, the accretor shrinks within its Roche lobe and the contact phase terminates. The reason for this is that the accretor's mass has increased to such a point that its thermal timescale is now equivalent to the mass transfer rate from the donor star as shown in \Cref{fig:kelvin_helmholtz_timescale}. The accretor is thus able to achieve thermodynamic equilibrium and return to its more compact state. This shrinking is not due to the heavier helium-rich material that has been accreted, although as we note below, post-contact the accretor with variable composition accretion is smaller in radius due to this effect, as shown in \Cref{fig:hrd_comparison_var_novarr} and \Cref{tab:binary_configuration_parameters}.

\subsection[Post-contact]{Post-contact (stages D--E)}\label{sec:post_contact_stages_d_e_}

There is a clear and consistent difference between the post-contact models. As shown in the inset of \Cref{fig:hrd_comparison_var_novarr}, the models diverge shortly before the termination of the contact phase. At this point, however, the die is already cast and the Rubicon is crossed. The variable composition accretion model has become \SI{0.01}{\dex}, or $\sim$\SI{1.16}{\kilo\kelvin} hotter than the non-variable model. This in turn leads the variable composition accretor to be hotter and eventually more luminous than its non-variable composition accretion counterpart, a theme that is reflected once the post-interaction behaviour is to be studied.

We thus examine the reasons for this apparent bifurcation. In \Cref{fig:composition_profile_evolution} we show the internal composition of the two stars in each binary at each timestep. Noticeably for stage (D), the accretion of helium-rich material has introduced a layer of helium \textit{atop} a layer of hydrogen, in the variable composition accretion case. This hydrogen is the original photosphere of the accretor, and the material has not yet mixed in at this stage, due to the continued injection of helium from the donor star.

Eventually, the extended envelope of the donor star cannot be supported, as it has lost a substantial amount of material. This leads to the collapse of the envelope, and the donor becomes a compact helium star. Over the contact phase, the average mass transfer efficiency has been 62.62~per~cent, with the donor star losing \SI{7.81}{\msun} of material and the accretor star gaining \SI{4.89}{\msun} of material. The balance is assumed to be lost to the \gls{ism}. The instantaneous efficiency, shown in \Cref{fig:main_evolution} and \Cref{fig:main_evolution_STANDARD}, peaks at 100~per~cent immediately prior to the start and end of the contact phase, and post-contact stays that way for $\sim\SI{5}{\kilo\year}$ after contact terminates as the system settles into a stable state with roughly constant radii, masses, and separation.

\subsection[Post-interaction and future history]{Post-interaction and future history (stage E--onwards)}\label{sec:future_history}

The future evolution of these stars will be determined by the counterplay of multiple complex systems \citep{Renzo:2021}. The key players in the future evolution are rotation-induced mixing and thermohaline mixing. Rotation-induced mixing will occur if the star spins closer to its critical rotation rate. We do not include this type of mixing in our code, as a 1D code simply lacks the dimensionality to accurately compute the mixing profile in detail.

The second effect is thermohaline mixing, which is the process by which an inverted mean molecular weight profile is stabilised by a temperature gradient \citep[e.g.,][]{Ulrich:1972, Kippenhahn:1980, Charbonnel:2007, Angelou:2011, Fraser:2022}, resulting in mixing where material with a heavier mean molecular weight sinks below lighter material. The primary concern with this effect is that the time-scale and therefore the efficiency of thermohaline mixing is highly uncertain. We display in \Cref{fig:hrd_comparison_var_novarr} the effect of varying the thermohaline mixing coefficient on the evolution of the variable composition accretion secondary. The evolution, both throughout the \gls{ms}, and crossing the Hertzsprung gap, is notably altered, with a change of approximately \SI{0.1}{\dex} in the temperature between maximum thermohaline mixing ($\alpha_\text{th}=0.1$) and no thermohaline mixing ($\alpha_\text{th}=0$; dashed black line). At the end of the evolution, as the thermohaline mixing models heat up, they undergo breathing pulses, which are of questionable physical origin \citep{Li:2021}, and can be removed by increasing the number of meshpoints in the model.

\section{Atypical abundance profiles}

As previously discussed, the accretor star has an atypical mean molecular weight profile. The discrepancy can be most seen in \Cref{fig:abundances_line_plot}. If thermohaline mixing is included, it would at this stage act to attempt to homogenise the star, with the heavier helium material effectively sinking to the core of the star. The effect of this will vary depending on the efficiency of thermohaline mixing, which is not conclusively known. To that end, the model of \citet{Eggleton:1972, Kippenhahn:1980, Stancliffe:2007} is employed, where the free parameter $\alpha_\text{th}$ controlling the strength of thermohaline mixing is allowed to vary. $\alpha_\text{th}=0$ corresponds to no thermohaline mixing, whereas $\alpha_\text{th}=1$ represents strong (virtually instantaneous) thermohaline mixing.

\section{Comparison between variable and non-variable composition accretion}

In \Cref{fig:hrd_comparison_var_novarr} we display a comparative HR diagram between the cases of variable and non-variable composition accretion. In each case an \SI[parse-numbers=false]{30+10}{\msun} binary was evolved with the only difference between the two being whether material was allowed to have variable composition accretion or the standard accretion prescription.

The first and most striking conclusion that we can draw from this result is that the companion is, in the variable composition accretion case, hotter and more luminous than the non-variable case. As previously mentioned, even near-instantaneous thermohaline mixing to homogenise the star was insufficient to return the accretor to a position on the HR diagram typified by non-variable accretion models.

Indeed, none of our single-star models are able to replicate the non-variable composition accretion model. However, a \SI{30}{\msun} model has a similar luminosity to the accretor but its surface temperature is \SI{0.15}{\dex} ($\sim\SI{9.6}{\kilo\kelvin}$) higher. This is due to the lower opacity of the helium-rich surface of the accretor. Similarly, we see that during the main sequence the accretor with variable composition accretion is also approximately \SI{0.15}{\dex} ($\sim\SI{11.3}{\kilo\kelvin}$) hotter in surface temperature than the non-variable composition accretion model.

\Cref{fig:hrd_comparison_var_novarr} shows that when thermohaline mixing is included we see this temperature difference decrease to approximately \SI{0.1}{\dex} ($\sim\SI{1.54}{\kilo\kelvin}$). Although thermohaline mixing has a bigger difference on the accretor tracks earlier in the main-sequence evolution than at the end of the main-sequence closer where the tracks are more similar in their evolutionary path.

\section{Implications}

We now examine some of the implications for this work and this model of mass transfer.

\subsection{Spectroscopic masses}

\alice

Throughout the history of binary evolution theory, a consistent thread has been the consistent underprediction of the mass predicted by models (the ``evolutionary mass'') vs the mass inferred from spectroscopic studies of stars (the ``spectroscopic mass''). Summaries are found in works such as \citet{Herrero:1992, Massey:2009, Bouret:2013, Massey:2013, McEvoy:2015, Markova:2018, Ramachandran:2018, Bestenlehner:2020}. The reason has been left as either deficiencies in the physics of model atmospheres \citep{Herrero:1992} or non-included or not well-understood physics \citep{Bestenlehner:2020}.

This phenomenon is not unique to one code or methodology. To name but four, it has been observed in:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item BONNSAI (work in \citealt{Bestenlehner:2020}, BONNSAI in \citealt{Schneider:2014}),
    \item the work of \citet{Maeder:1990} \citep{Herrero:1992},
    \item the Geneva tracks (work by \citealt{Markova:2018}, Geneva code by \citealt{Ekstrom:2012}), and
    \item the PoWR code (work by \citealt{Ramachandran:2018}, PoWR code in \citealt{Grafener:2002, Hamann:2003, Sander:2015}).
\end{enumerate}

If luminosities are systematically incorrect, as discussed above, then there would be a corresponding systematic incorrect calculation of the mass; that is, a traditional mass-luminosity relationship may not hold. We show in \Cref{fig:mass_luminosity_relationship} the relationship between the evolutionary mass and luminosity for both variable and non-variable composition accretion cases, as evolved in \autoref{sec:future_history}. We also display this for integer mass single-star models. Unsurprisingly, the non-variable accretion case evolves like a \SI{24}{\msun} model -- the initial mass of the model when this single-star evolution began was \SI{23.71}{\msun}, and the abundance profile typifies a single star; since the mass transfer was hydrogen-dominated, the profile is not inverted and thus resembles a single star.

On the other hand, the variable accretion case would (if observed) initially appear to be more massive than a \SI{24}{\msun} star (despite being \SI{23.66}{\msun} initially), eventually appearing to be approximately \SI{21}{\msun} -- i.e., $\sim$\SI{3}{\msun} less massive than it actually would be. Thermohaline mixing suppresses this change somewhat, but even with stronger thermohaline mixing still ultimately results in an accretor that is systematically different to a non-variable composition accretion model.

Consequently, any masses inferred from spectroscopy or luminosity measurements may be an underestimate of the true (evolutionary) mass. This would affect, for example, the inferred masses of compact objects and thus may have implications for our understanding of the progenitors of gravitational waves.

\subsection{WN/O3 stars}

The spectral class of these stars is another interesting question. They are overluminous for their mass, have helium-rich surfaces, and are hotter than expected for their luminosity. Otherwise, the general behaviour of the post-interaction accretors evolved as single stars is generally typical of main-sequence stars. This suggests that they may be the elusive WN/O3 progenitors \citep{Massey:2023}. These stars have previously been suggested to be the helium \textit{donors} from a binary interaction \citep{Gotberg:2018, Smith:2018}.

Whether these are donor products or accretor products will require further investigation, though we note that their relative isolation compared to other massive stars \citep{Smith:2018} and apparent single-star nature \citep{Massey:2023} does suggest that they could be accretor stars.

\subsection{Type Ib/c supernovae}

The surface temperature of the accretor has important implications for the observations of SN progenitors. The donor in our presented model is massive enough to explode as a Type Ib/c SN. A number of observations exist of the pre-SN site of many Type Ib/c SNe \citep[e.g.][]{Eldridge:2013, Eldridge:2016}. One issue is that in many of the observations not only is the progenitor unseen, so too is a massive main sequence star companion. We note that the higher surface temperature of the accretor makes it more difficult to detect in the optical bands which normally exist for this work. The different structure also makes it more difficult to predict the impact of the SN on the companion \citep[e.g.,][]{Hirai:2018}. Thus another reason to study the accretors in more detail is to understand the progenitor detections of Type Ib/c SNe when so few companions have been detected in post-SN observations \citep[e.g.][]{Eldridge:2016}.

\subsection{Prevalence of helium-rich mass transfer}

The question naturally arises of the prevalence of such mass transfer. To this end we used the \gls{bpass} v2.2 binary evolution models and population results code suite \citep{Eldridge:2017, Stanway:2018}. We searched every one of the binary models where mass is donated from the primary to the secondary. We note in these models mass transfer efficiency is limited by the thermal timescale of the accretor.

In a BPASS population with $Z = 0.020$, we count every system where the initial mass of the donor is greater than \SI{7}{\msun} where the secondary accretes more than 0.1 per cent of its initial mass. For an instantaneous burst of \SI[print-unity-mantissa=false]{1e6}{\msun} with the fiducial BPASS initial mass function and initial binary parameter distribution \citep{Stanway:2018}, we predict that 3543 binary stars will undergo this phase of evolution, of which 34 per cent accrete more than 5 per cent of their initial mass. Of these 3543 models, 23 per cent accrete material, which reaches a maximum helium abundance of greater than 0.8. Thus, the evolution of such accretors is common and is of vital importance to understand.

\section{Chapter Summary}

We have exhibited a case study of a close \SI[parse-numbers=false]{30+10}{\msun} binary, in which mass transfer becomes dominated by helium. By contrasting this to a binary with hydrogen-dominated mass transfer, we have shown that this results in an atypical composition profile that produces unexpected accretors. These accretors have systematically different luminosity compared to their hydrogen-dominated alternatives, translating to a different (incorrect) inferred mass.

In summary, the key conclusions of this chapter are as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The mass transfer efficiency in this binary interaction evolves with time through the mass transfer phase. A contact phase of evolution limits the mass transfer efficiency because the secondary cannot accrete more material whilst it is filling its Roche Lobe. But later, once the contact phase ends, the accretor becomes massive enough for its thermal timescale to come close to the mass transfer timescale, mass transfer efficiency increases.
    \item If a close binary system has a helium star within it, it can only have been formed by binary interactions. The accretor is likely to have accreted some helium-rich material and thus have different evolution due to the unusual composition profile this causes.
    \item Accretor stars with a helium-rich surface layer on top of a hydrogen-rich lower envelope and helium-rich convective core can be very different to a single star of the same mass. In the case presented a \SI{24}{\msun} accretor star was \SI{0.17}{\dex} more luminous and $\sim\SI{11.3}{\kilo\kelvin}$ hotter than a single star of the same mass. This means in a binary system post-mass transfer, normal mass-luminosity relationships may not hold for accretor stars, and stellar masses may be overestimated from luminosity.
    \item When thermohaline mixing is included in these models the surface composition grade is quickly reduced. This leads to the accretor becoming slightly cooler but it remains overluminous for its mass.
    \item At Solar metallicity, we expect 23 per cent of all systems that go through mass transfer to accrete helium-rich material with a maximum helium abundance of greater than 0.8.
