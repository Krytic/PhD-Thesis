\lettrine[lines=3]{A}{rguably the greatest advancement} in modern astronomy was the direct detection of gravitational waves -- the rippling of space-time as a result of the motion of massive celestial bodies -- on September 14, 2015. The \gls{ligo} collaboration detected the chirp of merging binary black holes, with masses $m_1=\SI[parse-numbers=false]{36^{+5}_{-4}}{\msun}$ and $m_2=\SI[parse-numbers=false]{29^{+4}_{-4}}{\msun}$ \citep{Abbott:2016}. This detection sparked the so-called ``gravitational wave era'', with detections in \gls{o4} reading a cadence of hundreds per year.\footnote{From the start of \gls{o4} in May 2023 to the end of December 2024, approximately 167 unretracted significant (false alarm rate of $<$\SI{1}{\per\month}) detections, averaging to a cadence of $\sim\SI{107}{\per\year}$.} These detections afford researchers probes of various physical phenomena. For instance, these merger events are some of the most energetic events in the Universe -- GW150914 peaked at a gravitational-wave luminosity of \SI{3.6e56}{\erg\per\second} \citep{Abbott:2016}, compared with the typical energy release of a \gls{ccsne} of $\sim$\SI[print-unity-mantissa=false]{1e53}{\erg} \citep{Hartmann:1999} -- and the merger of binary neutron stars are the predominant formation channels for most of the elements heavier than iron \citep[see, among others,][]{Eichler:1989, Arcavi:2017, Pian:2017, Smartt:2017}.

In addition, the stochastic background of gravitational waves is a direct probe on early-Universe cosmology \citep{Christensen:2018}, though the frequency band that these gravitational waves span is between $10^{-9}$ and $\SI[parse-numbers=false]{10^{14}}{\hertz}$ \citep{Christensen:1992, Christensen:2018}, making detection of the stochastic background difficult. Indeed, it took eight years after the detection of gravitational waves for the stochastic background to be detected by the collaborative effort of \gls{nanograv} \citep{Agazie:2023}, the \gls{epta} \citep{Antoniadis:2023}, the \gls{ppta} \citep{Reardon:2023}, and the \gls{cpta} \citep{Xu:2023}.

Beyond the stochastic background, the vast majority of gravitational wave sources involve stars at some point in their lives -- whether that be the merger of \gls{co}, the detonation of \gls{ccsne}, or the rotation of an isolated massive source such as a \gls{ns} or \gls{bh}. In these cases stars are either the immediate sources of gravitational waves, or the progenitors of the objects that are the sources. Only in extreme cases, such as the merger of galaxies or the motion of supermassive black holes, are stars not directly involved in the generation of gravitational waves (and even then, this is debatable).

Ergo, any attempt at understanding gravitational waves must be founded in a strong theoretical understanding of stellar structure and evolution. Without theory, any observation is no more than a curiosity; it is with theoretical underpinning and models that insight can be gained. As most astrophysical sources of gravitational waves (i.e., not the stochastic background) are formed by the merger of two ``compact remnants'' -- black holes, white dwarfs, and neutron stars -- one must understand their formation and dynamics, and thus the evolution of progenitor stars into these remnants, as well.

Furthermore, a sound understanding of the evolution of stars has impacts beyond gravitational wave astronomy. As the interiors of stars are complex nuclear furnaces, they are responsible for producing most of the elements in the Universe heavier than Hydrogen \citep{Burbidge:1957}. In fact, of the elements lighter than Uranium, the only elements not substantially produced in stellar processes (such as through kilonovae or in supernovae) are technetium, promethium (which both lack stable isotopes),\footnote{There are technetium stars, and promethium has been observed in some atmospheres \citep{Cowley:2004}, however these processes do not generally pollute their environment.} boron, beryllium (which both are produced via cosmic ray fission), and some formation channels of lithium.

From the perspective of Universal building blocks, stars are also the focal point of planetary systems, and provide the theoretical right conditions for the formation of life. Without a solid theory of stellar evolution, one is unable to describe the likelihood of extraterrestrial life within the Universe.

\section{Observational astronomy}

\SI{2300}{\pc} is an order of magnitude less than the size of Galaxy, which is approximately \SI{26.8}{\kilo\pc} in diameter \citep{Goodwin:1998}, which in turn pales in comparison to the Local Group at $\sim$\SI{3}{\mega\pc} \citep{Redd:2018}, and the observable Universe at \SI{28.5}{\giga\pc} \citep{Bars:2010}. There are, in addition, $\sim1.7\times10^{22}$ stars in the observable Universe, assuming $1.7\times10^{11}$ galaxies, with approximately $10^{11}$ stars in each \citep{Marov:2015}. Thus, the birth, evolution, and death of stars is of fundamental importance to understanding the local structure and evolution of astrophysical objects on any scale.

Given the number of stars visible in the vast expanse of night, and their relatively stable positions in the sky, it is no surprise that they are a fixture in many religions and cultures around the world. Ancient Greece saw a difference in movement of some celestial objects, and called these {\greekscript πλανήτης}, which morphed into \textit{plan\=etes} and eventually the English word ``planets''. Babylonia knew of Halley's comet and tracked observations of it as late as 87 BCE \citep{Stephenson:1985}. Records of eclipses in Chinese culture exist as far back as 1600 BCE \citep{Fang:2015}. Knowledge of the movements of stars guided Polynesian wayfarers as they crossed the South Pacific \citep{Buck:1938, Hamacher:2022}, were embedded in the cultural mythos of First Nations peoples in Australia \citep{Noon:2022}, allowed them to forecast weather patterns \citep{Hamacher:2019}, and were plausibly recorded into oral histories \citep{Fuller:2017}. Even in the arts, the knowledge of the lunar perigee was written into \textit{Othello} by Shakespeare:

Despite this deep cultural connection to the stars, what we would now call Astronomy is a somewhat recent invention. Arguably conventional (and in some sense, ``correct'') astronomy arose in the 16th century CE with Copernicus proposing the heliocentric model of the solar system, which was confirmed by Galileo in the 17th century CE \citep{Carroll:2017} -- although Galileo's claims did result in his being convicted of heresy for 300 years.

The early 20th century saw a veritable renaissance in both the observational and theoretical spheres. On the one hand, developments such as the theories of Special Relativity in 1905 \citep{Einstein:1905}, \gls{gr} in 1917 \citep{Einstein:1917}, and the predictions of gravitational waves \citep{Poincare:1906, Einstein:1916} led to theoretical revolutions. Around this time, Arthur Eddington began working on determining the internal structure and evolution of stars \citep{Eddington:1916, Eddington:1920}, and \citet{Gamow:1928} put forth the idea of quantum tunneling allowing elements to overcomb the Coloumb barrier. On the other hand, the innovations of \citet{Payne:1925} in showing that stars are composed of mostly Hydrogen and Helium, \citet{Baade:1934} in distinguishing ``super-novae'' from ``common novae'', \citet{Chandrasekhar:1935} in establishing the idea of the ``Chandrasekhar mass'' (the limiting mass of a white dwarf), \citet{Hoyle:1946} in developing the foundation of the nuclear reaction of elements heavier than Hydrogen in stars, \citet{Salpeter:1955} in developing the \gls{imf}, \citet{Burbidge:1957} in truly developing the theory of stellar nucleosynthesis, \citet{Henyey:1955} and \citet{Hayashi:1961} in exploring the pre-main sequence evolution of stars were key to kickstarting entire branches of astronomy such as spectroscopy and computational astrophysics.

The pace of discovery of new and exotic astronomical phenomena has not slowed into the 21st century. Thorne-\.{Z}ytkow objects -- giant stars with Neutron star cores \citep{Thorne:1977} -- once only existed as theoretical curiosities, yet now have credible observational candidates in \citet{Levesque:2014}. The aforementioned gravitational waves were detected in 2015  \citep{Ligo_scientific_collaboration_and_virgo_collaboration:2016}, a century after they were demonstrated by \citet{Einstein:1916} to be a central prediction of \gls{gr}. More recently, \citet{Lau:2022} directly imaged 17 carbon-rich dust rings around WR140 with \gls{jwst}, enriching the \gls{ism} with carbon-rich compounds. In the coming years, the Vera C. Rubin Observatory \citep{Ivezic:2019} and \gls{lisa} are expected to vastly increase the number of short-period eclipsing double white dwarfs \citep{Burdge:2020}, with the latter able to detect gravitational waves with frequencies between \SI{0.1}{\milli\hertz} and \SI{1}{\hertz} \citep{Amaro-seoane:2012}, compared to the $\sim \SI{10}{\hertz}-\SI{1000}{\hertz}$ that \gls{lvk} can detect \citep{Schmitz:2021}. The stochastic gravitational-wave background was detected by \citet{Agazie:2023, Antoniadis:2023, Reardon:2023, Xu:2023}, via a search of pulsar-timing arrays.

Astronomy as a field has blossomed since the naked-eye era -- entering the era of the telescope, the era of the observatory, and now the gravitational wave era. The next era of astronomy is the big data era. With observations reaching sub-day cadences, a large array of theoretical models, and perhaps the development of ``on-demand'' detailed models is required.

\section{Stellar Modelling}

Modelling the evolution of a star is a nontrivial endeavour. Stars reach internal temperatures, pressures, and densities unsustainable on Earth. For instance, the core of a star is capable of nuclear fusion, which has not been sustainably recreated on Earth. Moreover, the density of Sirius B -- a \gls{wd} in the constellation of Canis Major -- is \SI{2.14e10}{\kg\per\meter\cubed} \citep{Barstow:2005} and the mean density of the Sun is $\sim\SI{1410}{\kg\per\meter\cubed}$ \citep{Eddy:1979}. On Earth, the highest density measured in a laboratory is $\sim\SI{27000}{\kg\per\meter\cubed}$, requiring pressures on the order of \SI{2.3}{\tera\pascal} \citep{Fratanduono:2020}, and observed for only nanoseconds.

It is nigh impossible to achieve and sustain these conditions to experimentally test any physical phenomena. That said, missions such as the Parker Solar Probe have made flyby missions into the atmosphere of the Sun, though broadening the sample size to two stars -- using Proxima Centauri, the nearest star to Earth aside from the Sun -- would require travelling at approximately 0.1$c$ for 40 years to reach. If instead we were to send the Parker Solar Probe travelling at a constant velocity of its top velocity of $\sim\SI{176471}{\meter\per\second}$, it would take approximately 7200 years to reach Proxima Centauri. Needless to say, physical experimentation with a star is difficult.

\subsection{Population Synthesis and Stellar Modelling}

Because of these factors, one often turns to computer simulations to model the interiors of stars. The question turns from physical experimentation to mathematical ones, and the idea of a \textit{stellar model} becomes useful. A stellar model is a calculation performed such that the internal structure (the present state of physical quantities) and evolution (how these quantities change over time) are determined. Computing these parameters is nontrivial. As will be developed in \autoref{chap:theoretical_background}, the simplest model of a star is a series of four coupled differential equations describing the internal pressure, temperature, luminosity, and mass distributions. However, including ``extra'' physics greatly increases the complexity of the problem; \citet{Henyey:1964} developed methods of solving the equations of structure and evolution that turn the problem into matrix inversion, at the cost of the central ``Henyey matrix'' being thousands of elements in size.

The diversity and complexity of stellar profiles, interiors, and characteristics discussed earlier demands a careful and varied approach. One cannot abstract away much of the important physics. Although it is common to use approximations such as spherical symmetry to reduce the dimensionality of the problem, and to only evaluate the equations of stellar structure at a fixed number of locations in this one remaining dimension, chosen sufficiently intelligently, a complex computational effort must be undertaken.

This problem is then magnified when attempting to extrapolate out to describe an entire population of stars, such as from a burst of stellar formation activity, or in a shared environment such as a globular cluster. It is inefficient to, for example, model all approximately $10^{11}$ stars in the Milky Way \citep{Marov:2015} as independent entities. One must develop a way to take a library of models and determine the large-scale behaviour this way.

Broadly speaking, there are three approaches generating a population of stars, all of which have both merits and drawbacks. These three methods are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Rapid Population Synthesis,
    \item Detailed Population Synthesis, and
    \item \gls{mhd} Simulations.
\end{enumerate}

Each of these approaches informs the other two, and all play important roles in stellar modelling. Although we focus on detailed methods, we will briefly outline all three approaches here.

\subsubsection{Detailed Population Synthesis}

A detailed population synthesis code computes pairings of detailed models. One can in principle examine the output models to determine at any point the progenitor history. For example, when recording the sources of compact object coalescenes, one can place a prior on the mass of the components at their respective \gls{zams} positions, to only record binaries with a certain mass, or who at some point undergo a particular kind of mass transfer. In other regimes, this would need to be done in post-processing, potentially by querying the input distribution also.

\subsubsection{Rapid Population Synthesis}

Rapid population synthesis methods exploit the statistical properties of stars in order to rapidly infer properties of populations of stars. However, they still require as input detailed stellar models -- for example, \codename{Compas} \citep{Team_compas_riley:2022} uses \codename{Mesa} \citep{Paxton:2011, Paxton:2013, Paxton:2015, Paxton:2018, Paxton:2019, Jermyn:2023} models as its input models. The clear limitation is that it is more difficult (though not impossible) to disentangle an input model track to determine the progenitor of a particular system.

\subsubsection[3D MHD Simulations]{\acrshort{mhd} Simulations}

\acrfull{mhd} and \gls{sph} simulations are the most detailed method of stellar modelling. The base principle is that the star is evaluated as a network of particles in a continuous media, and the evolution of the star is followed in theoretically atomic detail. For example, \codename{Phantom} \citep{Price:2018} is a \gls{sph} code which essentially models the star as a fluid, where the Lagrangian hydrodynamical equations are solved for in two-dimensional cells (termed ``particles''). Although these present the most detailed models, they are also the most time-intensive to solve. For a \gls{sph} code, the famous Navier-Stokes equation is invoked, which has no analytical solution but is computed in excruciating detail. As a result, most \gls{sph} models take weeks to run and simulate a star for kiloseconds of its lifetime, rather than for the millions (if not billions) of years detailed models are able to compute.

Though it may seem that they serve the same niche and are therefore competing to be the ``better'' method of stellar modelling, detailed 1D models and \gls{mhd}/\gls{sph} models exist harmoniously with each other. The advancements in \gls{mhd}/\gls{sph} modelling inform the evolution of 1D models in a process known as ``3-to-1'', and the computational flexibility of 1D models offers insights into where 3D models wish to target to evolve, in a lock-step advancement method.

\subsection{Research aims}

There are essentially three major themes guiding this research.

\begin{enumerate}[(I)]
	\item Can we methodologically overcome the typical cases in which stellar models fail:
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item \gls{rlof}
		\item \gls{cee}
		\item The helium flash
	\end{enumerate}

	\item Can we develop a testing regime for the natal supernova kick, which relies on more observations than just isolated pulsar velocities?

	\item What modifications are required to be made to our stellar modelling code in preparation for the next major \acrshort{bpass} release?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Thesis outline}

This thesis is, broadly, a tour of stellar evolution from \gls{zams} to the eventual merger of two compact objects, with a few omissions along the way (such as the moment of supernova). The thesis is structured as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item In \autoref{chap:theoretical_background}, we provide the necessary theoretical background for future chapters, critically examine the pieces of software and numerical methods employed in this work, and discuss the logistics of running this single-thread grid-based code as a multiprocessed code on a distributed cluster.
	\item In \autoref{chap:close_contact}, we present the results of our stellar evolution codes in the interesting case when both stars almost (or in some instances, do) touch. (Theme I)
    \item In \autoref{chap:he_mass_transfer}, we explore the regime in which mass transfer is assumed to become helium-rich, and the effects on the future evolution of these binaries. (Themes I, III)
    \item In \autoref{chap:population_properties}, we explore the population properties of binaries which underwent helium-rich mass transfer (Themes I, III).
	\item In \autoref{chap:natal_kicks}, we examine the kinematics of the post-supernova inspiral and the mechanics of the natal supernova kick. (Theme II)
	\item In \autoref{chap:conclusion}, we review the thesis and conclude. (Themes I, II, III)
	\item In \autoref{chap:appendix:code_samples}, we provide the \codename{Kaitiaki} code necessary to generate the models for certain stellar evolution plots throughout the thesis. (Themes I, III)
	\item In \autoref{chap:appendix:code_versions}, we list the codes employed and their versions.
	\item In \autoref{chap:ligo_contour_results}, we present period-eccentricity distributions for the \acrshort{lvk} \acrshort{o3} contour (Theme II)
	\item In \autoref{chap:param_config}, we list the default parameter choices we make for our stellar evolution models (Themes I, III).
\end{enumerate}
