On September 14, 2015, the \gls{ligo} Collaboration made the first direct detection of gravitational waves, from the merger of a binary black hole with masses $m_1=\SI[parse-numbers=false]{36^{+5}_{-4}}{\msun}$ and $m_2=\SI[parse-numbers=false]{29^{+4}_{-4}}{\msun}$ \citep{Abbott:2016}. This detection sparked the so-called ``gravitational wave era'', with detections in \gls{o4} reading a cadence of hundreds per year. These detections afford researchers probes of various physical phenomena. For instance, these merger events are some of the most energetic events in the Universe (GW150914 released over 50 times the energy of all stars in the observable Universe, per \citet{Abbott:2016}), and the merger of binary neutron stars are the predominant formation channels for most of the elements heavier than iron \citep[see, among others,][]{Eichler:1989, Arcavi:2017, Pian:2017, Smartt:2017}. In addition, the stochastic background of gravitational waves is a direct probe on early-Universe cosmology \citep{Christensen:2018}, though the frequency band that these gravitational waves span is between $10^{-9}$ and $\SI[parse-numbers=false]{10^{14}}{\hertz}$ \citep{Christensen:1992, Christensen:2018}, making detection of the stochastic background difficult \citep{Agazie:2023, Antoniadis:2023, Reardon:2023, Xu:2023}.

Any attempt at understanding gravitational waves must be founded in a strong theoretical understanding of stellar structure and evolution. Without theory, any observation is no more than a curiosity; it is with theoretical underpinning and models that insight can be gained. As most astrophysical sources of gravitational waves (i.e., not the stochastic background) are formed by the merger of two ``compact remnants'' -- black holes, white dwarfs, and neutron stars -- one must understand their formation and dynamics, and thus the evolution of progenitor stars into these remnants, as well.

Furthermore, a sound understanding of the evolution of stars has impacts beyond gravitational wave astronomy. As the interiors of stars are complex nuclear furnaces, they are responsible for producing most of the elements in the Universe heavier than Hydrogen \citep{Burbidge:1957}. In fact, of the elements lighter than Uranium, the only elements not produced in stellar processes (such as through kilonovae or in supernovae) are technetium, promethium (which both lack stable isotopes), boron, beryllium (which both are produced via cosmic ray fission), and some formation channels of lithium.

From the perspective of Universal building blocks, stars are also the focal point of planetary systems, and provide the theoretical right conditions for the formation of life. There are approximately 9000 stars visible in the night sky to the naked eye (with magnitude lower than 6.5), across both hemispheres \citep{Hoffleit:1964}, which are displayed in \autoref{fig:naked_night_sky}. The dark-adapted eye can see approximately as far as $\eta$ Carinae,\footnote{This is a debated topic; we use the definition that a ``star'' includes binaries and that the star is in \citet{Hoffleit:1964}, which rules out V762 Cas, the other commonly held ``farthest star''.} which is only approximately \SI{2300}{\pc} away.

\SI{2300}{\pc} pales in comparison to the size of the Galaxy, which is approximately \SI{26.8}{\kilo\pc} in diameter \citep{Goodwin:1998}, which in turn pales in comparison to the Local Group at $\sim$\SI{3}{\mega\pc} \citep{Redd:2018}, and the observable Universe at \SI{28.5}{\giga\pc} \citep{Bars:2010}. There are, in addition, $\sim1.7\times10^{22}$ stars in the observable Universe, assuming $1.7\times10^{11}$ galaxies, with approximately $10^{11}$ stars in each \citep{Marov:2015}. Thus, the birth, evolution, and death of stars is of fundamental importance to understanding the local structure and evolution of astrophysical objects on any scale.

However, this diversity and complexity of stellar profiles, interiors, and characteristics demands a careful and varied approach. One cannot abstract away much of the important physics. Although it is common to use approximations such as spherical symmetry to reduce the dimensionality of the problem, and to only evaluate the equations of stellar structure at a fixed number of locations in this one remaining dimension, chosen sufficiently intelligently, a complex computational effort must be undertaken.

Astronomy as a field has blossomed since the naked-eye era (a brief discussion of the vast astronomical history is given as \autoref{chap:history_of_astronomy}) -- entering the era of the telescope, the era of the observatory, and now the gravitational wave era. The next era of astronomy is the big data era. With observations reaching sub-day cadences, a large array of theoretical models, and perhaps the development of ``on-demand'' detailed models is required.

\section{Stellar Modelling}

Modelling the evolution of a star is a nontrivial endeavour. Stars as objects obey \acrfull{gr} owing to their masses and sizes, and reach internal temperatures, pressures, and densities unsustainable on Earth. For instance, the core of a star is capable of nuclear fusion, which has not been sustainably recreated on Earth. Moreover, the density of Sirius B -- a \gls{wd} in the constellation of Canis Major -- is \SI{2.14e10}{\kg\per\meter\cubed} \citep{Barstow:2005} and the mean density of the Sun is $\sim\SI{1410}{\kg\per\meter\cubed}$ \citep{Eddy:1979}. On Earth, the highest density measured in a laboratory is $\sim\SI{27000}{\kg\per\meter\cubed}$, requiring pressures on the order of \SI{2.3}{\tera\pascal} \citep{Fratanduono:2020}, and observed for only nanoseconds.

Because of these factors, one often turns to computer simulations to model the interiors of stars, as it is nigh impossible to achieve and sustain these conditions to experimentally test any physical phenomena. That said, missions such as the Parker Solar Probe have made flyby missions into the atmosphere of the Sun, though broadening the sample size to two stars -- using Proxima Centauri, the nearest star to Earth aside from the Sun -- would require travelling at approximately 0.1$c$ for 40 years to reach. If instead we were to send the Parker Solar Probe travelling at a constant velocity of its top velocity of $\sim\SI{176471}{\meter\per\second}$, it would take approximately 7200 years to reach Proxima Centauri. Needless to say, physical experimentation with a star is difficult.

\subsection{Population Synthesis Codes}

Broadly speaking, there are three approaches to modelling stars, all of which have both merits and drawbacks. These three methods are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Rapid Population Synthesis,
    \item Detailed Population Synthesis, and
    \item \gls{mhd} Simulations.
\end{enumerate}

Each of these approaches informs the other two, and all play important roles in stellar modelling. Although we focus on detailed methods, we will briefly outline all three approaches here.

\subsubsection{Detailed Population Synthesis}

Detailed stellar models explicitly solve the equations of hydrostatic equilibrium, usually via some form of matrix-inversion method. For instance, as will be discussed in \autoref{chap:numerical_methods_stars}, the Cambridge \codename{STARS} code inverts the Henyey matrix $\mat{H}=\pdv{\pqty{\text{equation } i}}{\pqty{\text{variable } j}}$. \codename{Mesa} \citep{Paxton:2011, Paxton:2013, Paxton:2015, Paxton:2018, Paxton:2019, Jermyn:2023} uses a Newton-Raphson solver in order to converge to a solution. In particular, we use a variant of the Cambridge \codename{STARS} code, which solves the equations of \textit{both} stars simultaneously.

\subsubsection{Rapid Population Synthesis}

Rapid population synthesis methods exploit the statistical properties of stars in order to rapidly infer properties of populations of stars. However, they still require as input detailed population synthesis models -- for example, \codename{Compas} \citep{Team_compas_riley:2022} uses \codename{Mesa} \citep{Paxton:2011, Paxton:2013, Paxton:2015, Paxton:2018, Paxton:2019, Jermyn:2023} models as its input models.

\subsubsection[3D MHD Simulations]{\acrshort{mhd} Simulations}

\acrfull{mhd} and \gls{sph} simulations are the most detailed method of stellar modelling. The base principle is that the star is evaluated as a network of particles in a continuous media, and the evolution of the star is followed in theoretically atomic detail. For example, \codename{Phantom} \citep{Price:2018} is a \gls{sph} code which essentially models the star as a fluid, where the Lagrangian hydrodynamical equations are solved for in two-dimensional cells (termed ``particles''). Although these present the most detailed models, they are also the most time-intensive to solve. For a \gls{sph} code, the famous Navier-Stokes equation is invoked, which has no analytical solution but is computed in excruciating detail. As a result, most \gls{sph} models take weeks to run and simulate a star for kiloseconds of its lifetime, rather than for the millions of years detailed models are able to compute.

Though it may seem that they serve the same niche and are therefore competing to be the ``better'' method of stellar modelling, detailed 1D models and \gls{mhd}/\gls{sph} models exist harmoniously with each other. The advancements in \gls{mhd}/\gls{sph} modelling inform the evolution of 1D models in a process known as ``3-to-1'', and the computational flexibility of 1D models offers insights into where 3D models wish to target to evolve, in a lock-step advancement method.

\subsection{Research aims}

There are essentially three major themes guiding this research.

\begin{enumerate}[(I)]
	\item Can we methodologically overcome the typical cases in which stellar models fail:
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item \gls{rlof}
		\item \gls{cee}
		\item The helium flash
	\end{enumerate}

	\item Can we develop a testing regime for the natal supernova kick, which relies on more observations than just isolated pulsar velocities?

	\item What modifications are required to be made to our stellar modelling code in preparation for the next major \acrshort{bpass} release?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Thesis outline}

This thesis is, broadly, a tour of stellar evolution from \gls{zams} to the eventual merger of two compact objects, with a few omissions along the way (such as the moment of supernova). The thesis is structured as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item In Chapter 2, we provide the necessary theoretical background for future chapters, critically examine the pieces of software and numerical methods employed in this work, and discuss the logistics of running this single-thread grid-based code as a multiprocessed code on a distributed cluster.
	\item In Chapter 3, we explore the regime in which mass transfer is assumed to become helium-rich, and the effects on the future evolution of these binaries. (Theme I, III)
	\item In Chapter 4, we present the results of our stellar evolution codes in the interesting case when both stars almost (or in some instances, do) touch. (Theme I)
	\item In Chapter 5, we examine the kinematics of the post-supernova inspiral and the mechanics of the natal supernova kick. (Theme II)
	\item In Chapter 6, we review the thesis and conclude. (Themes I, II, III)
	\item In Appendix A, we present a brief history of observational astronomy for the interested reader.
	\item In Appendix B, we provide the \codename{Kaitiaki} code necessary to generate the models for certain stellar evolution plots throughout the thesis. (Themes I, III)
	\item In Appendix C, we list the codes employed and their versions.
	\item In Appendix D, we present period-eccentricity distributions for the \acrshort{lvk} \acrshort{o3} contour (Theme II)
	\item In Appendix E, we list the default parameter choices we make for our stellar evolution models (Themes I, III).
\end{enumerate}
