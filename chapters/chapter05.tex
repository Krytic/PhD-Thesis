\lettrine[lines=3]{H}{aving established the nature} and specific cases of both overcontact binaries and helium-rich mass transfer, we are now in a position to discuss the implications of these phenomena into a larger population. We shall build to this by discussing four core aspects of the work performed so far, and observing the regions of the grid which allow these kinds of interactions.

The four core phenomena we examine are the distributions of the lengths of the contact phase (cf. \Cref{chap:close_contact}), multiply-interacting systems (cf. \Cref{sec:multiply_interacting}), reverse mass transfer (cf. \Cref{sec:reverse_MT}), and helium-rich mass transfer (cf. \Cref{chap:he_mass_transfer}).

\section{Helium-Rich mass transfer}

To determine the prevalence of helium-rich mass transfer, for every binary in our grid we track whether it becomes helium-dominant ($X_\text{He}>0.5$) whilst the donor is filling its Roche lobe. If this occurs, we assume that the streamed material is becoming helium rich and will lead to a binary of the type studied in \Cref{chap:he_mass_transfer}. The results of this are shown in \Cref{fig:he_rich_density}. There is somewhat of a critical value of mass ratio, where for systems with $q<0.5$ initially, helium rich mass transfer happens essentially independently of the initial period (within our grid range). There are still a handful of systems above this critical mass ratio, however they are rarer, and tend to be more metal-poor.

This metallicity dependence is not unsurprising, as the \citet{Vink:2001} mass-loss scheme does contain a dependence on the metallicity (cf. \Cref{eq:pre-wr-ml}) -- thus metal-poor binaries have weaker mass loss rates, allowing interaction to start later, and allowing the models with mass ratios closer to unity to exchange helium.
