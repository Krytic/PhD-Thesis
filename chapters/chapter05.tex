\customchapter{Detailed Models of Overcontact Binary Stars}{I can remember... sitting on a rooftop. Looking up and wondering what the stars were. I assumed I’d never know.}{Hoid}\label{chap:close_contact}

\lettrine[lines=3]{A}{s previously discussed, stars} undergoing mass transfer will generally experience \gls{rlof} during their lifetimes. In a binary where both stars are undergoing mass transfer, both stars may undergo \gls{rlof} simultaneously, resulting in a contact binary system in which both stars physically touch. Contact binaries are not uncommon -- they form one in every approximately 100 main-sequence stars \citep{Rucinski:1998} and 39 in every 100 binary systems \citep{Green:2023}.

Modelling their evolution generally offers insight into difficult-to-probe stellar phenomena and evolutionary phases, such as the common envelope phase of binary evolution, as well as providing vital insights into the immediate pre-merger environment of a binary system. They are characterised by extremely short periods -- typically with $P_\text{orb}\lesssim\SI{2}{\day}$, but potentially as low as $P_\text{orb}=\SI{85}{\minute}$ \citep{El-badry:2022} in theory or $\sim\SI{167}{\minute}$ in practice \citep{Koen:2022}. The lower bound of the period distribution is an element of some debate and ongoing research; \citet{Qian:2020} finds it to be $\SI{0.15}{\day}\sim\SI{200}{\minute}$, and \citet{Zhang:2023} finds it to be at $\SI{0.12}{\day}\sim\SI{170}{\minute}$. The contact binary phase of evolution can give rise to pre-supernova mergers, chemically abundant mass-transfer drastically altering the interior structure of the accretor (\Cref{chap:he_mass_transfer}), and other modified evolutionary behaviour of the binary due to interaction effects stronger than that of wider-period binaries.

As a result of this close interaction, contact binaries may also be a formation channel for stellar exotica such as Thorne-\.Zytkow objects (T\.ZOs), first proposed in \citet{Thorne:1977}, where the capture of a neutron star by a supergiant companion in a close binary causes the formation of a T\.ZO \citep{Taam:1978}. It is pertinent to note that these are not the \textit{sole} formation channels for such exotica -- for instance, a T\.ZO could be formed via a primary supernova kicking a nascent neutron star into its companion \citep{Leonard:1994, Podsiadlowski:1995} or via collisions in clusters \citep{Podsiadlowski:1995} -- but are instead potential formation channels.

Accurately modelling close-contact binaries, then, is of crucial importance to population synthesis models. Recent studies such as \citet{Menon:2021, Abdul-masih:2022, Sen:2022} have explored some of the close-contact binaries in the Magellanic Clouds. \citet{Menon:2021} for example finds that for both Magellanic Clouds the majority of contact binaries are expected to have total masses of under $\sim\SI{40}{\msun}$ and periods of less than $\sim\SI{1}{\day}$, and \citet{Abdul-masih:2022} finds that the sample of close-contact binaries that they analyse are likely to form with relatively longer periods than most systems.

Despite the importance of modelling close-contact binaries, only $\sim25$ massive close-contact binaries are known to exist \citep{Abdul-masih:2023}. The paucity of observations is a reflection of the difficulty of distinguishing these systems from a more massive single star, especially since the two stars would share a common temperature \citep{Kuiper:1941} and thus similar observational properties. Spectroscopic observations also do not constrain physical parameters of contact binaries as well as they do for regular binaries (or single stars); for instance \citet{Gazeas:2021} discusses the O’Connell effect and surface spots of the stars as being sources that modulate the luminosity of the binaries, but especially of the secondary.

\section{Observations of contact binaries}

\alice

Observations of contact binaries date back to the 1940s \citep{Kuiper:1941}, and in the past eighty years the catalogue has grown to somewhere between $\sim\num{42000}$ \citep{Petrosky:2021} and $\sim\num{86000}$ \citep{Qian:2020} contact binaries, representing between 26.7 and 54.8 per cent of all observed binaries (those from the Washington Double Star catalogue; \citealt{Mason:2001}). The binaries we study in this chapter are summarised in \Cref{tab:overcontact_binaries}, and were originally compiled in \citet{Abdul-masih:2022}. Their sample focused on measuring the period stability of contact binaries and is thus subset of the known sample. They rejected a handful of other non O+O overcontact binaries, for reasons that include short-period von Zeipel-Kozai-Lidov oscillations, incomplete photometric / radial velocity fits, non-\gls{ms} binaries, unknown separations, and irregular light curves.

Ages for the six binaries have not been published, though a soft restriction can be placed on their evolutionary status; since \citet{Abdul-masih:2022} compiles specifically \gls{ms} contact binaries, their interactions must take place whilst the progenitor stars are on their respective main sequences. Nevertheless, we present inferred ages based on matches to the \gls{bpass} v2.2 models in \Cref{tab:hoki_matches}.

\subsection{Progenitor history}

We now examine each of the overcontact systems in detail, providing observational context to each system. For each binary, we list a selection of key measurements, including the fillout factor for each star. A fillout factor is a measurement of the degree to which a system overfills its Roche lobes, with $1 < f < 2$ denoting an overcontact binary, and a high fillout factor representing a more swelled and in-contact system.

\subsubsection{LSS 3074}

The binarity of LSS 3074 \citep{Raucq:2017} was first observed in 1985 by \citet{Morrell:1990} with an observed period of \SI{2.19}{\day}, with its HeI and HeII spectra causing the authors to conclude that it was an O4f+ and O6-7 binary, with inclination-weighted masses of $M_1\sin^3 i = \SI{8}{\msun}$ and $M_2\sin^3 i = \SI{9}{\msun}$. With later measurements of the inclinations to be $i\approx\ang{24}$, these masses were refined to $M_1 = \SI{17.2}{\msun}$ and $M_2=\SI{14.8}{\msun}$. It is located behind the Coalsack Nebula, at a distance of \SI{2256}{\pc}, with a B-band magnitude of \SI{12.81}{\mag} \citep{Stephenson:1971}. It has a fillout factor $f=1.05$.

\subsubsection{MY Camelopardalis}

MY Camelopardalis \citep[MY Cam;][]{Lorenzo:2014} is a binary in the cluster Alicante 1, and its binarity was established in 2003 by \citet{Negueruela:2003}. It is likely to be a very massive merger progenitor, though is barely in contact with $f=1.01$ \citep{Abdul-masih:2022}.

\subsubsection{SMC 108086}

OGLE SMC-SC10 108086 \citep[SMC 108086;][]{Hilditch:2005} is a O9 + O9.5/B0 binary. It is a system deeply in contact, with $f=1.70$ \citep{Hilditch:2005, Abdul-masih:2022} -- the deepest known -- and has a short period of $P=\SI{0.88}{\day}$. It is located at a distance of \SI{56.5}{\kilo\pc}, in the \gls{smc}.

\subsubsection{TU Muscae}

TU Muscae \citep[TU Mus;][]{Penny:2008} is an O8V + B0V binary discovered by \citet{Oosterhoff:1928, Oosterhoff:1930}. Its binarity was confirmed as late as 1971, in \citet{Knipe:1971}. The system was profiled in \citet{Andersen:1975}, who concluded that, based on comparison to theoretical models by \citet{Stothers:1972, Stothers:1973}, the system was of solar composition ($Z=0.021\approx\si{\zsun}$) and on the main sequence with $X_\text{H}=0.739$. It has a fillout factor of $f=1.12$.

\subsubsection{V382 Cygni}

V382 Cygni \citep[V382 Cyg;][]{Martins:2017} is an O6.5 + O6 binary whose components are oblate spheroids (deduced from the $\beta$-Lyrae light curves; \citealt{Pearce:1952}). It was originally discovered by \citet{Morgenroth:1935, Petrov:1946}. It has a fillout factor of $f=1.10$.

\subsubsection{VFTS 352}

VFTS 352 was detected in the VLT-FLAMES survey of the Tarantula Nebula \citep{Evans:2011} and is the most recent detection in our sample. The Tarantula Nebula / 30 Doradus is a large H(II) region in the \gls{lmc}. The progenitor of VFTS 352 is likely an equal mass, short period binary with no strong helium or nitrogen surface abundances \citep{Abdul-masih:2019}, which may be due to biases in the CNO abundances, or similar, in that atmospheric study. It has a fillout factor of $f=1.28$.

\subsection{Data matching with BPASS v2.2}\label{sec:BPASS_data_matching}

We perform our data matching using the \codename{Hoki} code \citep{Stevance:2020a}. The search parameters are shown in \Cref{tab:overcontact_binaries}. We query the \gls{bpass} v2.2.1 dataset and record whether or not they enter the (widened) parameter values. Parameters are widened as some of the periods are tighter than the \gls{bpass} bins and thus can be missed in the search.

The matching values \codename{Hoki} finds are shown in \Cref{tab:hoki_matches}. The primary models are also shown on \Cref{fig:hoki_matches_HRD_primaries}. We also report in the table the inferred ages at match, which lie between $\mu_{\log(\text{age})}=6.13$ (for MY Cam) and $\mu_{\log(\text{age})}=7.14$ (for TU Mus).

We note two key caveats in our search criteria. Our search error bars are widened, in some cases substantially. The known parameters in \citet{Abdul-masih:2022} are so tightly constrained that the parameter grid spacing in \gls{bpass} becomes significant, and thus the search bars must be widened to account for this.

Secondly, luminosities are not uniformly reported for the companions across the grid. In particular, the companion luminosity for VFTS 352 is unreported, and thus we approximate it using the Stefan-Boltzmann law,

\begin{equation}
    \frac{L}{L_\odot} = \left(\frac{R}{R_\odot}\right)^2 \left(\frac{T}{T_\odot}\right)^4,\label{eq:sb_law}
\end{equation}

where $T_\odot=\SI{5778}{\kelvin}$. This induces two assumptions: that the companion is a perfect blackbody, and that it is spherical. The first assumption is typically assumed regardless, and the secondary is a necessary condition of our 1D stellar evolution approach. Although the star is, physically, distorted with a fillout factor of $f=1.28$, the key assumption underpinning 1D stellar evolution is spherical symmetry. Nevertheless, the illustration on the \gls{hrd} is for illustrative purposes only. Since the binaries in this sample are eclipsing binaries, the most confident parameters we could fit to are the masses and radii.

The full future history is completed by examining the remnants. To account for the uncertainties present in the natal kick prescription (\Cref{chap:natal_kicks}), we consider three kick prescriptions: the \citet{Hobbs:2005} and \citet{Richards:2023} kicks from \Cref{chap:natal_kicks}, and the bimodal \citet{Verbunt:2017} kick. The \citet{Hobbs:2005} and \citet{Richards:2023} kicks were described in \Cref{chap:natal_kicks}, and the \citet{Verbunt:2017} kick motivates its choice with a second peak at lower kick velocities to account for phenomena such as electron-capture supernovae and \glspl{ussne}. It samples the kick velocity from a modified Maxwellian distribution, a la \citet{Hobbs:2005}, though the distribution is given by

\begin{equation}
    P(v_\text{k})~\mathrm{d}v_\text{k} = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} v_\text{k}^2\left[\frac{w}{\sigma_1^3}\exp\left(-\frac{v_\text{k}^2}{2\sigma_1^2}\right)+\frac{1-w}{\sigma_1^3}\exp\left(-\frac{v_\text{k}^2}{2\sigma_1^2}\right)\right],
\end{equation}

where $\sigma_1=\SI{75}{\km\per\s}$ and $\sigma_2=\SI{316}{\km\per\s}$ are the distribution parameters and $w=0.42$ is the weight of the lower peak. For each binary, we compute the post-supernova kick parameters $M_1, M_2, a_0$ and $e_0$ in the same way as in \Cref{chap:natal_kicks}, and use these to calculate the coalescence time in \si{\giga\year}.

For each system, we record the type of binary left after the secondary supernova -- \gls{nsbh}, \gls{bhwd}, \gls{nswd}, \gls{bns}, \gls{bbh}. If the mean eccentricity is less than zero or strictly greater than one, we record it as an Unbound system, and present the percentage of systems in which the binary unbinds. If the binary remains bound, we present the coalescence time, which is allowed to be arbitrarily large (with the largest being \SI{1.75}{\peta\year}). In practice arbitrarily large coalescence times are likely unphysical, with \unit{\peta\year} coalescence times approaching the end of the stelliferous era. Nevertheless, we include them as predictions made by the integration of the equations of \citet{Peters:1964}.

We assume that a white dwarf is left if the \gls{CO_core} mass of the star is less than \SI{1.38}{\msun}, the total mass of the star is less than \SI{1.5}{\msun}, or if core carbon burning does not occur. Otherwise, we assume that remnants with $M_\text{rem} \leq \SI{2.5}{\msun}$ leave behind a \gls{ns}, and otherwise a \gls{bh} is left.

We present these results in \Cref{chap:overcontact_binary_remnants}. We can see a number of extremely low-mass \glspl{wd}, the lightest of which are \SI{0.28}{\msun}, barely above the current lowest-mass \gls{wd} \citep[\SI{0.17}{\msun};][]{Kilic:2007}. It is possible that these may be numerical artefacts, and that the supernova has either destroyed the star leaving effectively no remnant, or that the stars merged during the supernova event. This would be compounded by the stars appearing to be unbound, with high frequency. The \codename{tui} code does not record whether a merger occurs during the supernova, so this is not possible to test given the current architecture of the data.

\section{Forward evolution}\label{sec:forward_evolution}

Having now established the \gls{zams} parameters for our binaries, we can take these as input to our \codename{stars} code to determine their future history. For each matched model, we extract the masses and periods, and then use these as input to our stellar modelling code. In particular, we use the modifications earlier described that enable helium-rich mass transfer.

We examine the models at three periods. The first is the fiducial period, as reported from the \codename{Hoki} code. The second is a set of binaries with identical parameters except for periods \SI{0.1}{\dex} higher, and the third is binaries with periods \SI{0.1}{\dex} lower. Our aim, in evaluating the expanded period grid, is to determine the sensitivity of the grid to this parameter choice, given that the major aim of this chapter is to investigate, effectively, short-period binaries.

The three models all fulfil their matching criteria at similar ages to their v2 counterparts. The differences can be ascribed to two key factors. Firstly, the \gls{bpass} v2 ages were computed as matches on the HR diagram to primarily the donor, as \gls{bpass} v2 models the secondary as a point mass during the pre-supernova evolution of the donor. Secondly, the \gls{zams} periods were \SI{0.1}{\dex} wider than the ones used in the computation of the \codename{stars} model, and originate in our lower-period grid as described at the beginning of \Cref{sec:forward_evolution}.

\section{Discussion}

The most obvious limitation is that we were able to only produce three matching systems on one of the six close-contact binaries. \Cref{fig:matches_MVP_primaries} shows that, especially for SMC 108086 and MY Camelopardalis, the parameter space exploration was insufficient and that, were the space widened, more matches may be able to be found.

The initial matches for systems, as listed in \Cref{tab:overcontact_binaries}, was performed as matches to positions on an \gls{hrd}, which presents difficulty. First, as contact binaries, the secondary luminosity is difficult to constrain, and the assumption of the Stefann-Boltzmann law (\Cref{eq:sb_law}) is unlikely to hold proper. This resulted in intentionally wide search bars, to encapsulate this uncertainty, but also resulted in binaries that are unlikely to actually match the search parameters.

This entire study also relies on the \gls{bpass} v2 models to evaluate the \gls{zams} parameters of our models. Whilst tested against observations, this means uncertainties in \gls{bpass} are also inherent in uncertainties of our models. For the most part, this is of little concern. The key limitation of our approach is the fact that \gls{bpass} models the accretor as a point mass prior to the primary supernova \citep{Eldridge:2017}, contributing further to the aforementioned difficulty in constraining the search parameters for the accretor.

In order to obviate these limitations, a wide grid in both primary and secondary mass could be computed. By doing so, one removes the dependence on the \gls{bpass} secondary grid and inherent uncertainties arising from \citet{Hurley:2002}, at the computational expense of having to search a much larger range of models for matches.

\section{Chapter Summary}

We started by compiling the six contact binaries in the sample of \citet{Abdul-masih:2022}. We matched these binaries to their \gls{zams} parameters using the \gls{bpass} and \codename{hoki} codes, finding between one and ten viable sets of initial parameters to each model. We also found their mean ages to be between \SI{1.34}{\mega\year} and \SI{13.8}{\mega\year}, depending on the model.

By forward-evolving them using the \codename{stars} code, we found that 14 model tracks matched, for LSS 3074, TU Mus, and V382 Cyg. We found that these matches were short compared to the lifetime of the binary, generally existing for $\sim\SI{200}{\kilo\year}$ -- $\sim\SI{600}{\kilo\year}$. Of the fourteen matches, we found that only three matched the observed masses and radii of both components in the binary, all for TU Mus on the shortest grid. We conjectured that this may be an artifact of the parameter space explored, and that a wider grid would enable us to find closer matches.
